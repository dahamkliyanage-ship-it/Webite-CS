Matrix Diagonalization

The reason for diagonalizing an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is to simplify the structure of $A$ so that, in some ways, it reflects the simple computational characteristics of a diagonal matrix.

Definition(Similar Matrices): Suppose $A$ and $B$ are two square matrices of the same order. Then $A$ is similar to $B$ if there exists an invertible matrix $P$ such that
$$ B = P^{-1}AP $$
  • Theorem: If the matrix $A$ is similar to the matrix $B$ then $B$ is similar to $A$.
  • Theorem: If the matrix $A$ is similar to the matrix $B$ then $A$ and $B$ have the same eigenvalues.
Definition(Diagonalizable Matrix): A matrix is diagonalizable if it is similar to a diagonal matrix. That is, if $A$ is diagonalizable, then there exists a diagonal matrix $D$ and an invertible matrix $P$ such that $D = P^{-1}AP$.

How to Diagonalize:
Suppose an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ has $n$ eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \cdots, \lambda_n$ (which need not be distinct). Also suppose $X_1, X_2, \cdots, X_n$ are $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors corresponding to these eigenvalues.

Then construct the $n \times n$ matrix $P$ where the $i^{\text{th}}$ column of $P$ is the eigenvector $X_i$. We have $D = P^{-1}AP$, which results in the diagonal matrix:
$$ D = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \lambda_n \end{pmatrix} $$
where the eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ is in the $i^{\text{th}}$ position on the diagonal. Here $P$ is called the diagonalizing matrix.

Remark: A diagonalizing matrix is not unique because it depends on the order in which the eigenvectors of $A$ are arranged to form its columns.
Application: Computing Positive Integer Powers

For a given matrix $A$, its diagonalization greatly simplifies calculating powers of $A$. Suppose $A$ can be diagonalized as $D = P^{-1}AP$. Rearranging this gives $A = PDP^{-1}$.

Squaring the matrix yields:
$$ A^2 = (PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1}) = PD(P^{-1}P)DP^{-1} = PD^2P^{-1} $$
In general, for any positive integer $n \in \mathbb{N}$:
$$ A^n = PD^nP^{-1} $$
Since $D$ is a diagonal matrix, $D^n$ is simply computed by raising each diagonal entry to the $n^{\text{th}}$ power.
Orthogonal Diagonalization for Symmetric Matrices

Remark. If $A$ is an $n \times n$ real symmetric matrix with distinct eigenvalues, then it has $n$ mutually orthogonal linearly independent eigenvectors.

Hence, the diagonalizing matrix $P$ formed by using the normalized eigenvectors is an orthogonal matrix (meaning $P^{-1} = P^T$). In this special case, the diagonalization can be written as:
$$ D = P^T A P $$

The Cayley-Hamilton Theorem

The Cayley-Hamilton theorem is an important result in linear algebra that simplifies matrix computations. It allows us to express higher powers of a matrix $A$ in terms of its lower powers, and it can also be utilized to elegantly compute the inverse of a matrix.

Theorem(Cayley-Hamilton theorem):

If $P(\lambda)$ is the characteristic polynomial of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, then $A$ satisfies its own characteristic equation. That is:
$$ P(A) = O $$
where $O$ is the $n \times n$ zero matrix.
Worked Example: Using Cayley-Hamilton to find an Inverse

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.

Step 1: Find the characteristic polynomial.
$$ P(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) = \begin{vmatrix} 1-\lambda & 2 \\ 3 & 4-\lambda \end{vmatrix} = (1-\lambda)(4-\lambda) - 6 = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda - 2 $$
Step 2: Apply the Cayley-Hamilton Theorem.
Substitute $A$ for $\lambda$. The theorem states that:
$$ A^2 - 5A - 2I = O $$
Step 3: Solve for $A^{-1}$.
Multiply the entire equation by $A^{-1}$:
$$ A^{-1}(A^2 - 5A - 2I) = A^{-1}O $$ $$ A - 5I - 2A^{-1} = O $$ $$ 2A^{-1} = A - 5I \implies A^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}(A - 5I) $$
This provides a direct formula to compute the inverse using only matrix addition and scalar multiplication!
