Introduction

In standard limits, proving sequence convergence typically requires knowing the destination target $L$ beforehand in order to evaluate $|u_{n}-L|<\epsilon$[cite: 272]. However, in real-world numerical computing or iterative physical solvers, the exact true answer is almost never known ahead of time[cite: 272]. A Cauchy sequence serves as an internal sanity test: rather than measuring the distance to an unknown destination $L$, it measures the distance between the sequence's own terms ($|u_{n}-u_{m}|<\epsilon$)[cite: 272]. If the terms progressively bunch closer together, the sequence is guaranteed to settle into a definite point, provided the mathematical space is "complete" (has no missing holes)[cite: 272].

1. Cauchy Sequences & Complete Sets

Definitions & Foundational Properties[cite: 272, 273]

  • Definition of a Cauchy Sequence: A sequence $u_{n}$ is Cauchy if and only if for all $\epsilon>0$, there exists an index $N$ such that for any $m, n > N$, the distance $|u_{n}-u_{m}|<\epsilon$[cite: 272].
  • Property 1: Every converging sequence is Cauchy[cite: 272]. The triangle inequality proves that $|u_{n}-u_{m}| \le |u_{n}-L| + |u_{m}-L| < \frac{\epsilon}{2} + \frac{\epsilon}{2} = \epsilon$[cite: 272].
  • Property 2: Every Cauchy sequence is bounded[cite: 272]. By setting $\epsilon=1$, all terms beyond an index $N$ are within a distance of 1 from each other, allowing a maximum bound $M$ to be established for the entire sequence[cite: 272].
  • Definition of a Complete Set: A set $A$ is Complete if every Cauchy sequence defined on $A$ converges to a point that lies inside $A$[cite: 272]. The real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ are complete, whereas the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ are not[cite: 272, 273].
Worked Exercises: Testing Cauchy Sequences[cite: 274, 275]

Exercise 1.4: Disproving a False Claim
Claim: Does $u_{n+1}-u_{n}\rightarrow0$ as $n\rightarrow\infty$ imply that $u_{n}$ is Cauchy[cite: 274]?
Counterexample: The harmonic series partial sums $u_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}$[cite: 274]. While the consecutive gap $|u_{n+1}-u_{n}|=\frac{1}{n+1}\rightarrow0$, checking indices $m=2n$ gives $|u_{2n}-u_{n}| \ge \frac{1}{2}$[cite: 274]. Thus, no matter how large $N$ is, the distance does not fall below $\epsilon = \frac{1}{4}$, proving the claim false[cite: 274].

Exercise 1.5: Contraction Mapping Principle
If $|u_{n+1}-u_{n}|\le\alpha|u_{n}-u_{n-1}|$ with $0<\alpha<1$, then $u_{n}$ is Cauchy[cite: 274]. By bounding the tail sum with a geometric series, the distance $|u_{n}-u_{m}| < \frac{d\cdot\alpha^{m-1}}{1-\alpha}$, which goes to 0 as $m\rightarrow\infty$[cite: 274, 275].

Testing Completeness of Sets

Examples of Complete and Incomplete Sets[cite: 275, 276]

  • The Set of Integers $\mathbb{Z}$: Complete[cite: 275]. A Cauchy sequence of integers eventually forces $|u_{n}-u_{m}| < \frac{1}{2}$, which is only possible if $u_{n}=u_{m}$[cite: 275]. A constant sequence converges trivially within $\mathbb{Z}$[cite: 275].
  • The Set of Irrational Numbers $\mathbb{R}-\mathbb{Q}$: Not Complete[cite: 275]. The sequence $u_{n}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{n}$ consists entirely of irrationals but converges to 0, which is rational[cite: 275]. The sequence escapes the set[cite: 275].
  • The Open Interval (0,1): Not Complete[cite: 275]. The sequence $u_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}$ is inside (0,1) and converges to 0[cite: 275]. However, $0\notin(0,1)$, meaning the limit escapes the boundary[cite: 275].
  • The Closed Interval [0,1]: Complete[cite: 275]. Since $0\le u_{n}\le1$, taking limits preserves the inequalities ($0\le \lim u_{n}\le1$), ensuring the limit point $L$ remains within [0,1][cite: 275, 276].

2. Subsequences & The Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem

Definitions & The Three Equivalent Pillars[cite: 276, 277]

  • Subsequence Limit Theorem: If a sequence converges to $L$, then every one of its subsequences also converges to $L$[cite: 276].
  • Monotone Subsequence Theorem: Every sequence has at least one monotonic subsequence[cite: 276].
  • Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem: Every bounded sequence has a converging subsequence[cite: 276].
  • Cauchy Subsequence Theorem: If a Cauchy sequence contains a subsequence that converges to $L$, then the entire sequence converges to $L$[cite: 276].

The Three Equivalent Pillars of Real Analysis (TFAE)[cite: 276]:
For any non-empty subset $A\subset\mathbb{R}$, the following are equivalent[cite: 276]:
  1. $A$ satisfies the Completeness Axiom Property (bounded subsets have a supremum/infimum in $A$)[cite: 276].
  2. The Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem holds on $A$[cite: 276].
  3. $A$ is Cauchy Complete[cite: 276].
