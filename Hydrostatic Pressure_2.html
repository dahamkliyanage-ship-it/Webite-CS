2.0 Hydrostatic Pressure

Exploration of fluid statics, understanding pressure at a point via Pascal's Law, absolute versus gauge pressure, and the principles of pressure measurement using manometers and mechanical gauges.

2.1 Static Fluid Pressure (Hydrostatic Pressure)

Hydrostatic pressure is defined as the pressure exerted by a fluid when it is in a state of equilibrium (at rest). In this state, there are no shear stresses present within the fluid; the only forces acting on fluid surfaces are normal forces.

Pressure ($P$) = Normal Force ($F$) / Area ($A$)

$$P = \lim_{\delta A \to 0} \frac{\delta F}{\delta A}$$

Key Attributes:

  • Dimensions: \([P] = [\delta F] / [\delta A] = [MLT^{-2}] / [L^2] = [ML^{-1}T^{-2}]\)
  • Units: N/m2 = Pa (Pascal). Also measured in Bar (1 Bar = 105 N/m2).
  • Nature: Pressure is a scalar quantity. The force vector \(d\vec{F}\) acting on a differential area \(d\vec{A}\) is given by \(d\vec{F} = P \cdot d\vec{A}\).

2.2 Characteristics of Hydrostatic Pressure

1. Pascal's Law

Pascal's Law states: The hydrostatic pressure at a point in a fluid at rest is the same in all directions.

$$P_x = P_y = P_z = P_\theta = P$$

Proof of Pascal's Law (Wedge Element)

Proof Strategy: Consider a small wedge-shaped fluid element containing point A in equilibrium.

  • Forces in x-direction: \(P_x (\Delta y \cdot \Delta z) - P_\theta (\Delta y \cdot \Delta s) \sin\theta = 0\). Since \(\sin\theta = \frac{\Delta z}{\Delta s}\), this simplifies to \(P_x = P_\theta\).
  • Forces in z-direction: \(P_z (\Delta x \cdot \Delta y) - P_\theta (\Delta y \cdot \Delta s) \cos\theta - \frac{1}{2}\Delta x \cdot \Delta y \cdot \Delta z \cdot \rho g = 0\). As the element shrinks to a point (\(\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta z \to 0\)), the weight term approaches zero. Since \(\cos\theta = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta s}\), this simplifies to \(P_z = P_\theta\).

Therefore, \(P_x = P_z = P_\theta\). Since the angle \(\theta\) is arbitrary, pressure is the same from all directions.

Note: For moving fluids where shear stresses exist, the average normal pressure is considered: \(P = \frac{P_x + P_y + P_z}{3}\).

2. Variation of Hydrostatic Pressure (Hydrostatic Principle)

Let \(P\) be the pressure at a point \(A = (x, y, z)\) in the fluid. Using partial differentiation:

$$dp = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x}dx + \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}dy + \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}dz$$

For an incompressible fluid in equilibrium, it can be shown that:

  • \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial x} = 0\) (No pressure variation horizontally).
  • \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial z} = -\rho g\) (Pressure varies vertically).

Integrating \(dp = -\rho g \cdot dz\) gives \(P = -\rho g z + C\).

Piezometric Pressure: Rearranging gives \(P + \rho g z = C\). The term \(P + \rho g z\) is known as the Piezometric Pressure ($P^*$). In a homogeneous, incompressible fluid at rest, the Piezometric pressure is constant.

3. Pressure Difference Between Two Points

Considering two points in a fluid: Point 1 at depth \(z_1\) and Point 2 at depth \(z_2\).

\(P_2 - P_1 = -\rho g (z_2 - z_1)\)

If we define \(h\) as the depth measured downwards (\(h = z_1 - z_2\)), the equation becomes:

$$P_2 = P_1 + \rho g h$$

Conclusions:

  1. Hydrostatic pressure varies linearly with depth.
  2. When points are on the same horizontal plane (\(h=0\)), \(P_1 = P_2\). Surfaces of constant pressure are called Isobars, and they are horizontal.

2.3 Absolute and Gauge Pressure

Atmospheric Pressure ($P_{atm}$): The pressure exerted by atmospheric air. Standard atmospheric pressure at sea level is:

  • 1.033 kgf/cm2 (1 kgf ≈ 9.81 N)
  • 1.013 × 105 N/m2
  • 1.013 Bars (1 Bar = 105 N/m2)
  • 1 Atmosphere (atm)
  • 14.7 psi

Pressure Measurement Scales

Absolute Pressure: Pressure measured with respect to a perfect vacuum. Always positive.

Gauge Pressure: Pressure measured with respect to atmospheric pressure. It can be positive or negative (vacuum pressures).

$$Absolute\ Pressure = Atmospheric\ Pressure + Gauge\ Pressure$$

$$P_{abs} = P_{atm} + P_{gauge}$$

Pressure Head

Pressure Head is the height of liquid volume that will produce the pressure at a point.

$$Pressure\ Head\ (h) = \frac{P}{\rho g} = \frac{P}{\gamma}$$

Example: For an atmospheric pressure of 1.013 × 105 N/m2, the pressure head in terms of water (ρ = 1000 kg/m3) is h = (1.013 × 105) / (1000 × 9.81) = 10.33 m of water.

2.4 Pressure Measurements

Various devices are used to measure fluid pressure.

1. Barometer

Used to measure atmospheric pressure.

2. Piezometer

An open-ended vertical tube connected to a vessel/pipe containing a liquid. Height of the liquid column \(h\) gives the pressure head: \(P = \gamma h\).

  • Advantages: Simple, measures small pressure differences.
  • Disadvantages: For liquids only. Requires long tubes to measure even moderate pressures.

3. Manometer

A U-shaped tube containing a heavy "manometer liquid" (e.g., mercury) connected to a vessel. The displacement $h$ of the manometer liquid column measures the pressure.

Applying the Hydrostatic Principle:

Equating pressures on the left and right limbs at the lowest fluid interface:

$$P_{Left} = P_{Right}$$

$$P_A + \rho_{fluid} g y = P_{atm} + \rho_{manometer} g h$$

If we want Gauge Pressure at A, we set $P_{atm} = 0$:

$$P_{A} = \rho_{manometer} g h - \rho_{fluid} g y$$

Differential Manometers are used to measure the pressure difference between two points. Advantage: Measures pressure difference in gases and liquids easily.

4. Mechanical Pressure Gauges (e.g., Bourdon Gauge)

Consists of a metal tube of oval cross-section bent to a shape of a 'C'. One end is fixed, and the other is closed and free to move. When internal pressure increases, it straightens, moving a pointer over a calibrated scale.

  • Advantages: Easy to use, wide range of pressures.
  • Disadvantages: Less accurate, needs regular calibration.
