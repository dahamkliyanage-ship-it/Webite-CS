The Theorem

The most crucial property connecting these two special functions allows us to convert complex Beta integrals into simple factorial calculations via the Gamma function.

Relation between Beta and Gamma Functions

For any real numbers $m > 0$ and $n > 0$, the relationship is strictly defined as:
$$ B(m, n) = \frac{\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(m + n)} $$

Proof by Double Integral and Polar Coordinates

Proof Outline

We use the alternative form of the Gamma function: $\Gamma(k) = 2\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}x^{2k-1}dx$.

Consider the product of two Gamma functions:
$$ \Gamma(m)\Gamma(n) = \left( 2\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}x^{2m-1}dx \right) \left( 2\int_0^\infty e^{-y^2}y^{2n-1}dy \right) $$
Combine them into a double integral over the first quadrant:
$$ = 4 \int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty e^{-(x^2+y^2)} x^{2m-1} y^{2n-1} dx dy $$
Transform to polar coordinates: Let $x = r\cos\theta$ and $y = r\sin\theta$. The area element becomes $dx dy = r dr d\theta$, and the limits are $0 \le r < \infty$ and $0 \le \theta \le \pi/2$.
$$ = 4 \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^\infty e^{-r^2} (r\cos\theta)^{2m-1} (r\sin\theta)^{2n-1} r dr d\theta $$
Group the $r$ terms and $\theta$ terms separately:
$$ = \left( 2 \int_0^\infty e^{-r^2} r^{2(m+n)-1} dr \right) \left( 2 \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^{2n-1}\theta \cos^{2m-1}\theta d\theta \right) $$
Recognize that the first integral is precisely $\Gamma(m+n)$, and the second integral is the trigonometric form of the Beta function, $B(m,n)$ (or $B(n,m)$).
$$ \Gamma(m)\Gamma(n) = \Gamma(m+n) B(m,n) $$
Rearranging this equation yields the fundamental theorem:
$$ B(m,n) = \frac{\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(m+n)} $$

Added Worked Examples

Example A: Evaluating a Trigonometric Integral

Evaluate the integral $\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^4\theta \cos^5\theta d\theta$.

Solution: We use the trigonometric form of the Beta function:
$$ \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^{2m-1}\theta \cos^{2n-1}\theta d\theta = \frac{1}{2} B(m, n) $$
Matching the exponents:
  • $2m - 1 = 4 \implies 2m = 5 \implies m = \frac{5}{2}$
  • $2n - 1 = 5 \implies 2n = 6 \implies n = 3$
The integral becomes $\frac{1}{2} B\left(\frac{5}{2}, 3\right)$. Using the relation to Gamma functions:
$$ \frac{1}{2} B\left(\frac{5}{2}, 3\right) = \frac{1}{2} \frac{\Gamma(5/2)\Gamma(3)}{\Gamma(11/2)} $$
Calculate the individual Gamma values:
  • $\Gamma(3) = 2! = 2$
  • $\Gamma(5/2) = \frac{3}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \sqrt{\pi} = \frac{3}{4}\sqrt{\pi}$
  • $\Gamma(11/2) = \frac{9}{2} \cdot \frac{7}{2} \cdot \frac{5}{2} \cdot \frac{3}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \sqrt{\pi} = \frac{945}{32}\sqrt{\pi}$
Substitute these back into the expression:
$$ \text{Result} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{(\frac{3}{4}\sqrt{\pi})(2)}{\frac{945}{32}\sqrt{\pi}} = \frac{3/4}{945/32} = \frac{3}{4} \cdot \frac{32}{945} = \frac{24}{945} = \frac{8}{315} $$
Example B: Computing a Standard Beta Value

Evaluate $B(4, 5)$.

Solution: Since both parameters are positive integers, we can use the factorial property $\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!$:
$$ B(4, 5) = \frac{\Gamma(4)\Gamma(5)}{\Gamma(4 + 5)} = \frac{\Gamma(4)\Gamma(5)}{\Gamma(9)} $$ $$ = \frac{3! \cdot 4!}{8!} $$ $$ = \frac{6 \cdot 24}{40320} = \frac{144}{40320} = \frac{1}{280} $$
