1. Rolle's Theorem & The Mean Value Theorems

Foundational Theorems[cite: 37, 38]

  • Rolle's Theorem: Let $f$ be continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$ with $f(a)=f(b)$[cite: 37]. Then there exists at least one $c\in(a,b)$ such that $f^{\prime}(c)=0$[cite: 37].
    Proof Sketch: By EVT, $f$ attains a max $M$ and min $m$[cite: 37]. If $M=m$, the function is constant and $f^{\prime}=0$ everywhere[cite: 37]. If $M \ne m$, since endpoints are equal, an extremum occurs at an interior point $c$[cite: 37]. By Fermat's Theorem, $f^{\prime}(c)=0$[cite: 37].
  • The Mean Value Theorem (Lagrange MVT): Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$[cite: 37]. There exists $c\in(a,b)$ such that $f^{\prime}(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$[cite: 37].
    Proof Sketch: Construct a secant line error function $h(x)=f(x)-f(a)-\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a)$[cite: 37]. Since $h(a)=h(b)=0$, apply Rolle's theorem to $h(x)$[cite: 37]. Differentiating $h$ yields the formula[cite: 37, 38].
  • Cauchy's Generalized Mean Value Theorem: Let $f, g$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$ with $g^{\prime}(x)\ne0$[cite: 38]. There exists $c\in(a,b)$ such that $\frac{f^{\prime}(c)}{g^{\prime}(c)}=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}$[cite: 38].

2. Rigorous L'Hôpital's Rule

The Formal Theorem & Analytical Derivation[cite: 38, 39]

L'Hôpital's Rule ($0/0$ Form): Suppose $f(a)=g(a)=0$, $f, g$ are continuous on $[a, a+\delta)$ and differentiable on $(a, a+\delta)$, with $g^{\prime}(x) \ne 0$[cite: 38]. If $\lim_{x\rightarrow a^{+}}\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{g^{\prime}(x)}=L$, then $\lim_{x\rightarrow a^{+}}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=L$[cite: 38].

Proof via Cauchy MVT: For a given $\epsilon$, we know the ratio of derivatives is within $\epsilon$ of $L$ on some interval $(a, a+\delta_1)$[cite: 39]. For any $x$ in a valid subinterval, applying Cauchy's MVT provides a point $c$ such that $\frac{f^{\prime}(c)}{g^{\prime}(c)} = \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{g(x)-g(a)} = \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$[cite: 39]. Since $c$ is within the $\delta_1$ bounds, the ratio of the functions is also bounded strictly within $\epsilon$ of $L$, satisfying the limit definition[cite: 39].

Step-by-Step Indeterminate Form Evaluations

Exercise 2.2: Evaluate $\lim_{x\rightarrow0}\frac{x^{2}\sin(1/x)}{\sin x}$[cite: 39]
Taking derivatives yields $\cos(1/x)$ terms which oscillate and fail the limit requirement[cite: 39]. Instead, split algebraically: $\lim_{x\rightarrow0}(\frac{x}{\sin x})\cdot(x\sin\frac{1}{x})$[cite: 39, 40]. The first part goes to 1, the second goes to 0 (via Squeeze Theorem)[cite: 40]. Product rule gives $1 \cdot 0 = 0$[cite: 40].

Exercise 2.3: Evaluate $\lim_{x\rightarrow0^{+}}\frac{\ln x}{\cot(\pi x)}$[cite: 40]
Form: $\frac{-\infty}{\infty}$[cite: 40]. Apply L'Hôpital's Rule:
$\lim_{x\rightarrow0^{+}}\frac{1/x}{-\pi\csc^2(\pi x)} = \lim_{x\rightarrow0^{+}}\frac{-\sin^2(\pi x)}{\pi x} = \lim_{x\rightarrow0^{+}}[-\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x}\cdot\sin(\pi x)]$[cite: 40]. Since $\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x} \rightarrow 1$ and $\sin(\pi x) \rightarrow 0$, the limit evaluates to 0[cite: 40].

Exercise 2.4: Evaluate $\lim_{x\rightarrow\pi/4}(\tan x)^{\tan(2x)}$[cite: 40]
Form: $1^{\pm\infty}$[cite: 40]. Convert to exponential form $y$, so $\ln y = \frac{\ln(\tan x)}{\cot(2x)}$, yielding $[0/0]$[cite: 40].
Deriving numerator: $\frac{1}{\sin x\cos x}$[cite: 40]. Deriving denominator: $-\frac{1}{2\sin^2x\cos^2x}$[cite: 40].
The ratio simplifies to $-\sin(2x)$, which limits to $-1$ at $\pi/4$[cite: 40]. By continuity of the exponential, the final answer is $e^{-1} = 1/e$[cite: 40, 41].

3. Higher Derivatives & The Second Derivative Test

Definitions & Second-Order Extrema[cite: 41, 42]

  • Second-Order Linear Approximation: If $f^{\prime\prime}$ exists on $(a, b)$, then $f(x)=f(c)+f^{\prime}(c)(x-c)+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)}{2}(x-c)^{2}$ for some intermediate point $\zeta$[cite: 41].
  • Second Derivative Test: Let $f^{\prime}(c)=0$ and $f^{\prime\prime}$ be continuous at $c$[cite: 41].
    • If $f^{\prime\prime}(c)>0$, $c$ is a strict local minimum[cite: 41].
    • If $f^{\prime\prime}(c)<0$, $c$ is a strict local maximum[cite: 41].
    • If $f^{\prime\prime}(c)=0$, the test is inconclusive[cite: 41].
  • Proof via Remainder Expansion: Since $f^{\prime}(c)=0$, $f(x)-f(c) = \frac{f^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)}{2}(x-c)^2$[cite: 41]. If $f^{\prime\prime}(c)>0$, continuity ensures $f^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)>0$ nearby[cite: 41]. Since $(x-c)^2$ is positive, $f(x)-f(c)>0 \Rightarrow f(x)>f(c)$, making $c$ a local minimum[cite: 41, 42].
