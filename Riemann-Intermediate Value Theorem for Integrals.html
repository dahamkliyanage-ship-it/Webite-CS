Intermediate Value Theorem for Integrals

Theorem(Mean Value Theorem for Definite Integrals):

If $f$ is a continuous function on $[a,b]$, then for at least one $x \in (a,b)$ we have:
$$ f(x) = \frac{1}{b - a} \int_a^b f $$
Proof:
Let $M$ and $m$ be the maximum and minimum values of $f$ on $[a,b]$. If $M = m$, then $f$ is a constant function and $f(x) = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f$ for all $x \in [a,b]$.

Otherwise, $m < M$, then there exist distinct $x_0$ and $y_0$ in $[a,b]$ satisfying $f(x_0) = m$ and $f(y_0) = M$. Since each function $M - f$ and $f - m$ is non-negative and not identically 0, then $\int_a^b m < \int_a^b f < \int_a^b M$. Thus,
$$ m < \frac{1}{b - a} \int_a^b f < M $$
and by the Intermediate Value Theorem for continuous functions, we have $f(x) = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f$ for some $x$ between $x_0$ and $y_0$. Since $x \in [a,b]$, this completes the proof.

Generalized Intermediate Value Theorem

Theorem(Generalization of Intermediate Value Theorem):

Let $f, g : [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous functions and $g(t) \ge 0, \forall t \in [a,b]$. Then $\exists$ at least one $x \in [a,b]$ such that:
$$ \int_a^b f(t)g(t)dt = f(x) \int_a^b g(t)dt $$
That is,
$$ f(x) = \frac{\int_a^b f(t)g(t)dt}{\int_a^b g(t)dt} $$
Note: This is a generalization for I.V.T. By taking $g(t) = 1$, we may obtain the standard I.V.T for integrals.
Proof:
Assume $M$ and $m$ are the maximum and minimum values of $f(x)$ on $[a,b]$. Since $g(x) \ge 0$:
$$ mg(x) \le f(x)g(x) \le Mg(x) $$
Integrating across the inequality gives:
$$ m \int_a^b g(t)dt \le \int_a^b f(t)g(t)dt \le M \int_a^b g(t)dt $$
Hence,
$$ m \le \frac{\int_a^b f(t)g(t)dt}{\int_a^b g(t)dt} \le M $$
By the I.V.T. for continuous functions, this value must be taken by the function $f$ on $[a,b]$.

Interchanging Limits and Integrals

An important question concerns when one can interchange limits and integrals. i.e., when is $\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_a^b f_n(x)dx = \int_a^b f(x)dx$ true?

If the $f_n$'s are continuous and converge uniformly to $f = \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n$ on $[a,b]$, then $f$ is continuous and the statement holds. It turns out that if each $f_n$ is just Riemann integrable and $f_n \to f$ uniformly, then $f$ is Riemann integrable and the statement holds.

Theorem(Uniform Convergence):
Let $f_n$ be a sequence of R.I. functions on $[a,b]$ and suppose $f_n \to f$ uniformly on $[a,b]$. Then $f$ is R.I. on $[a,b]$ and:
$$ \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_a^b f_n(x)dx = \int_a^b f(x)dx $$
Proof:
Let $\epsilon > 0$. Since $f_n \to f$ uniformly, there exists $N$ such that $|f_n(x) - f(x)| < \frac{\epsilon}{2(b-a)}$ for all $n \ge N$. Using partitions and Riemann sums $U(f - f_N, P)$ and $L(f - f_N, P)$, one can bound the difference by $\epsilon$, proving $f - f_N$ is R.I. Hence $f = (f - f_N) + f_N$ is R.I. Consequently, the integral of the difference is bounded by $\frac{\epsilon}{2} < \epsilon$, proving the limit.

Pointwise Convergence

What happens if $f_n \to f$ pointwise on $[a,b]$? One problem is that $f$ need not be integrable even if it is bounded and each $f_n$ is integrable. Nevertheless, important theorems exist for sequences converging pointwise.

Example(Failure of Pointwise Convergence):
Let $\{q_k : k \in \mathbb{N}\}$ be an enumeration of the rationals in $[0, 1]$. Define $f_n : [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f_n(x) = 1$ if $x = q_k$ for $k \le n$, and $0$ otherwise.
Each $f_n$ is Riemann integrable (it differs from 0 at finitely many points). However, $f_n \to f$ pointwise to the Dirichlet function $f$, which is not Riemann integrable.
Theorem(Bounded/Dominated Convergence Theorem):
Suppose $(f_n)$ is a sequence of integrable functions on $[a,b]$ and $f_n \to f$ pointwise where $f$ is an integrable function. If there exists an $M > 0$ such that $|f_n(x)| \le M$ for all $n$ and all $x \in [a,b]$, then:
$$ \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_a^b f_n(x)dx = \int_a^b f(x)dx $$
Theorem(Monotone Convergence Theorem):
Suppose $(f_n)$ is a sequence of integrable functions on $[a,b]$ such that $f_1(x) \le f_2(x) \le \dots$ for all $x \in [a,b]$. Suppose also that $f_n \to f$ pointwise where $f$ is an integrable function on $[a,b]$. Then:
$$ \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_a^b f_n(x)dx = \int_a^b f(x)dx $$

Examples and Applications

Show that $\frac{11}{24} \le \int_0^{1/2} \sqrt{1 - x^2}dx \le \frac{11\sqrt{3}}{36}$.

Example:
Show that $\frac{1}{\pi} \le \int_0^1 \frac{\sin \pi x}{1 + x^2} dx \le \frac{2}{\pi}$.
Example:
The function $f_n : [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by $f_n(x) = \frac{n + \cos x}{ne^x + \sin x}$ converges uniformly on $[0, 1]$ to $f(x) = e^{-x}$. By Theorem:
$$ \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_0^1 \frac{n + \cos x}{ne^x + \sin x} dx = \int_0^1 e^{-x} dx = 1 - \frac{1}{e} $$
Example:
Find $\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_{\pi/2}^\pi \frac{\sin(nx)}{nx} dx$. (This sequence of integrals converges to 0 by applying bounding theorems).
Added Worked Example: Applying the standard IVT for Integrals
Find the average value of the function $f(x) = x^2$ on the interval $[0, 3]$, and find the point $c$ guaranteed by Theorem.

Step 1: Calculate the average value.
$$ f_{avg} = \frac{1}{3 - 0} \int_0^3 x^2 dx = \frac{1}{3} \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^3 = \frac{1}{3} \left( \frac{27}{3} \right) = 3 $$
Step 2: Find $c$ such that $f(c) = 3$.
$$ c^2 = 3 \implies c = \sqrt{3} $$
Since $\sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$ is in the interval $[0, 3]$, this verifies the theorem.
