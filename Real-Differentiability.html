Introduction

Continuity simply guarantees that a curve has no breaks or teleportations: you can draw it without lifting your pencil[cite: 31]. Differentiability requires something much stronger: smoothness[cite: 31]. If you zoom in infinitely close to a point on the curve, the graph must flatten out into a single, unambiguous straight tangent line[cite: 31]. If the curve has a sharp spike, a corner, or an infinite vertical cliff, the left and right approach speeds disagree or blow up, meaning instantaneous velocity cannot be defined[cite: 31].

1. Differentiability: Definitions & Fundamental Properties

Definitions & One-Sided Derivatives[cite: 31, 32]

  • Definition of Differentiability: A function $f$ is differentiable at $a$ iff the limit $f^{\prime}(a)=\lim_{x\rightarrow a}\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$ exists as a finite real number[cite: 31].
  • Existence Criterion: $f$ is differentiable at $a$ if and only if both one-sided derivatives exist and are exactly equal to the same finite real value[cite: 31].
  • Differentiability Implies Continuity: If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$[cite: 31, 32]. Note: The reverse is not true.
  • Derivative Arithmetic Rules: If $f, g$ are differentiable, they obey the Sum, Difference, Product, Quotient, and Chain rules[cite: 32]. (e.g. Chain Rule: $(f\circ g)^{\prime}=(f^{\prime}\circ g)\cdot g^{\prime}$)[cite: 32].
Worked Exercises: Testing Differentiability[cite: 33, 34]

Exercise 3.1: $f(x) = |x|$ at $x=0$[cite: 33]
Right derivative: $\lim_{x\rightarrow0^{+}}\frac{x}{x}=1$[cite: 33]. Left derivative: $\lim_{x\rightarrow0^{-}}\frac{-x}{x}=-1$[cite: 33]. Since $1 \ne -1$, $f$ is not differentiable at 0[cite: 33].

Exercise 3.1: $f(x) = x|x|$ at $x=0$[cite: 33, 34]
Limit of difference quotient: $\lim_{x\rightarrow0}\frac{x|x|-0}{x-0}=\lim_{x\rightarrow0}|x|=0$[cite: 34]. Both left and right limits equal 0, thus $f$ is differentiable at 0[cite: 34].

Exercise 3.3: Right derivative of $\sqrt{x}$ at $x=0$[cite: 34]
${f^{\prime}}_{+}(0)=\lim_{x\rightarrow0^{+}}\frac{\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{0}}{x-0}=\lim_{x\rightarrow0^{+}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}=\infty$[cite: 34]. Since the limit is not a finite real number, it is not right-differentiable at 0[cite: 34].

2. Local Extremum & Critical Points

Definitions & Necessary Conditions[cite: 32, 33]

  • Local Maximum: $\exists\delta>0, \forall x (|x-a|<\delta \Rightarrow f(x) \le f(a))$[cite: 32].
  • Critical Point: A point $a$ is a critical point iff $f^{\prime}(a)=0$ or $f$ is not differentiable at $a$[cite: 33].
  • Interior Extremum Theorem (Fermat's Theorem): Let $f$ be differentiable at an interior point $a$[cite: 33]. If $a$ is a local extremum, then $f^{\prime}(a)=0$[cite: 33].
    Proof Sketch: Bounding from the right gives $f^{\prime}(a)\le0$, and from the left gives $f^{\prime}(a)\ge0$[cite: 33]. By trichotomy, $f^{\prime}(a)$ must equal 0[cite: 33].

3. Selected Proof Walkthroughs

Exercise 3.4: $f^{\prime}(x)$ driving $f(x)$ to infinity[cite: 34, 35]
Prove that if $\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}f^{\prime}(x)=L>0$, then $\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}f(x)=\infty$[cite: 34].
If the limit of the derivative is $L>0$, we can choose a value $c$ such that $0 < c < L$[cite: 34]. There exists some $N>0$ where for all $x > N$, $f^{\prime}(x) > c$[cite: 34]. By the Mean Value Theorem, $f(x)-f(N) = f^{\prime}(\xi)(x-N)$ for some $\xi \in (N,x)$[cite: 34]. Since $f^{\prime}(\xi) > c$, we know $f(x) > f(N) + c(x-N)$[cite: 34, 35]. As $x\rightarrow\infty$, that right-hand side grows to $\infty$, forcing $f(x)$ to infinity by comparison[cite: 35].

Exercise 3.6: Darboux's Theorem[cite: 35, 36]
Statement: Let $f$ be differentiable on $[a,b]$ with $f^{\prime}(a)\ne f^{\prime}(b)$. If $u$ is strictly between them, there exists $c\in(a,b)$ such that $f^{\prime}(c)=u$[cite: 35].
Proof: Define auxiliary function $g(x) = ux - f(x)$[cite: 35]. By EVT, $g$ attains a maximum on $[a,b]$[cite: 35]. Evaluating the derivatives at the boundaries shows $g$ is increasing at $a$ and decreasing at $b$, meaning the maximum cannot be at either boundary[cite: 35]. The maximum must occur at an interior point $c$[cite: 35]. By Fermat's theorem, $g^{\prime}(c) = 0$[cite: 35]. Since $g^{\prime}(c) = u - f^{\prime}(c) = 0$, we have $f^{\prime}(c) = u$[cite: 36].
