Skew Lines

Definition:

Two non-parallel lines in a 3-space that do not intersect are called skew lines.

Unlike parallel lines (which lie in the exact same plane and never meet) or intersecting lines (which share a common point), skew lines exist in different planes and are unique to three-dimensional coordinate geometry.

Distance between two skew lines

The distance between two skew lines is the shortest distance between them, which is uniquely defined as the length of the common perpendicular line segment that connects both lines.

Symmetric Equations of the Lines

It is given that the straight lines are expressed symmetrically as:
$$ \frac{x - x_0}{a_0} = \frac{y - y_0}{b_0} = \frac{z - z_0}{c_0} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{x - x_1}{a_1} = \frac{y - y_1}{b_1} = \frac{z - z_1}{c_1} $$
These lines are skew lines, where point $A = (x_0, y_0, z_0)$ lies on the first line, and point $B = (x_1, y_1, z_1)$ lies on the second line.

Figure 1: Geometric representation of skew lines, points $A$ and $B$, and their common unit normal vector $\underline{n}$.
Distance Formula Derivation

The shortest distance between the two skew lines is given by the magnitude of the scalar projection of the vector $\overrightarrow{AB}$ onto the unit normal vector $\underline{n}$ shared perpendicularly by both lines:
$$ \text{the distance} = |\overrightarrow{AB} \cdot \underline{n}| $$
where $\underline{n}$ is a unit normal vector perpendicular to both direction vectors. That is computed by taking the cross product of the two direction vectors and normalizing it:
$$ \underline{n} = \frac{\langle a_0, b_0, c_0 \rangle \times \langle a_1, b_1, c_1 \rangle}{|\langle a_0, b_0, c_0 \rangle \times \langle a_1, b_1, c_1 \rangle|} $$

Detailed Mathematical Example

Example: Finding Distance Between Skew Lines

Find the shortest distance between the skew lines defined by:
Line 1: $\frac{x}{1} = \frac{y-1}{1} = \frac{z}{1}$
Line 2: $\frac{x-1}{2} = \frac{y}{1} = \frac{z}{0}$

Step 1: Identify points $A$ and $B$ and direction vectors.
  • Point on Line 1: $A(0, 1, 0) \implies \underline{r_A} = \langle 0, 1, 0 \rangle$
  • Point on Line 2: $B(1, 0, 0) \implies \underline{r_B} = \langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle$
  • Vector connecting them: $\overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 1-0, 0-1, 0-0 \rangle = \langle 1, -1, 0 \rangle$
  • Direction vector 1: $\underline{v_0} = \langle 1, 1, 1 \rangle$
  • Direction vector 2: $\underline{v_1} = \langle 2, 1, 0 \rangle$
Step 2: Compute the cross product of the direction vectors.
$$ \underline{v_0} \times \underline{v_1} = \begin{vmatrix} \underline{i} & \underline{j} & \underline{k} \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \end{vmatrix} $$ $$ = \underline{i}(0 - 1) - \underline{j}(0 - 2) + \underline{k}(1 - 2) = \langle -1, 2, -1 \rangle $$
Step 3: Find the magnitude of the cross product vector.
$$ |\langle -1, 2, -1 \rangle| = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + 2^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 1} = \sqrt{6} $$
Step 4: Form the unit normal vector $\underline{n}$ and compute the distance.
$$ \underline{n} = \frac{\langle -1, 2, -1 \rangle}{\sqrt{6}} $$ $$ \text{Distance} = |\overrightarrow{AB} \cdot \underline{n}| = \left| \langle 1, -1, 0 \rangle \cdot \frac{\langle -1, 2, -1 \rangle}{\sqrt{6}} \right| $$ $$ = \frac{|(1)(-1) + (-1)(2) + (0)(-1)|}{\sqrt{6}} = \frac{|-1 - 2|}{\sqrt{6}} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{6}} = \frac{\sqrt{6}}{2} \text{ units} $$
