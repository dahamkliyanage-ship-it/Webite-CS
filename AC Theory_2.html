3.0 Alternating Current (AC) Theory

Comprehensive study of alternating current, sinusoidal waveforms, phasor representation, impedance, admittance, and complex AC power analysis.

3.1 Introduction to Waveforms

A waveform is obtained by plotting the instantaneous value of a time-varying quantity (such as voltage or current) against time. Waveforms can be periodic or non-periodic.

  • Periodic Waveform: The same pattern repeats exactly after a specific time interval, $T$. This time $T$ is the periodic time (Period). The frequency is $f = 1/T$, measured in Hertz (Hz).
  • Alternating Waveform: A specific type of periodic waveform where the magnitude and direction continuously change with time, alternating between positive and negative values across the zero axis.
  • Sinusoidal Waveform: An alternating waveform that perfectly follows a mathematical sine or cosine function. It is the fundamental waveform of modern electrical grids.

General Equation of a Sinusoid:

$$v(t) = V_m \sin(\omega t + \Phi)$$

Where: $V_m$ = Maximum/Peak value, $\omega$ = Angular frequency ($2\pi f$ in rad/s), and $\Phi$ = Phase angle shift.

3.2 Magnitude of Alternating Voltages and Currents

Because AC waveforms continuously change magnitude, engineers use specific statistical measures to quantify them for power calculations.

  • Peak Value ($V_m$ or $I_m$): The absolute maximum instantaneous value measured from the zero reference line.
  • Peak-to-Peak Value ($V_{p-p}$): The total voltage difference between the maximum positive peak and the maximum negative peak. For a pure sine wave, $V_{p-p} = 2V_m$.
  • Average Value ($V_{av}$): The arithmetic average over half a cycle (a full cycle average is zero for a symmetrical AC wave). For a pure sinusoid: $V_{av} = \frac{2}{\pi} V_m \approx 0.637 V_m$.
  • Effective or RMS Value ($V_{rms}$): The equivalent DC voltage that would dissipate the exact same amount of thermal power across a given resistor. It is the most important measure in AC analysis. For a pure sine wave: $V_{rms} = \frac{V_m}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707 V_m$.
Critical Standard: Unless explicitly stated otherwise, AC voltages and currents specified on equipment or in problem statements (e.g., "A 230V, 50Hz supply") are always RMS values.

3.3 Phasor Representation

Analyzing AC circuits using trigonometric sine and cosine functions becomes incredibly tedious when adding multiple waves. Instead, we use Phasors.

A phasor is a rotating vector in the complex plane used to represent a time-varying sinusoidal quantity. The length (magnitude) of the phasor is proportional to the RMS value of the waveform, and its angle represents the phase shift ($\Phi$) relative to a standard reference (usually $0^\circ$).

The Complex Phasor Plane

Complex Number Representation (Operator $j$)

Phasors are mathematically manipulated using complex numbers in either Polar form ($A\angle\Phi$) or Rectangular/Cartesian form ($A_x + jA_y$), where the operator $j = \sqrt{-1}$.

  • Multiplying a phasor by $j$ explicitly rotates it by exactly $+90^\circ$ (counterclockwise).
  • Multiplying a phasor by $-j$ rotates it by exactly $-90^\circ$ (clockwise).

3.4 Impedance and Admittance

In AC circuits, resistance is generalized to a complex quantity called Impedance ($Z$), measured in Ohms ($\Omega$). Impedance restricts current flow and causes phase shifts.

$$Z = \frac{V}{I} = R + jX$$

Where $R$ is the Real part (Resistance) and $X$ is the Imaginary part (Reactance).

  • Pure Resistor: Current and voltage are perfectly in phase. $Z = R + j0 = R\angle0^\circ$.
  • Pure Inductor: Voltage leads current by $90^\circ$. Inductive Reactance $X_L = \omega L$. Therefore, $Z = 0 + jX_L = X_L\angle90^\circ$.
  • Pure Capacitor: Current leads voltage by $90^\circ$. Capacitive Reactance $X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C}$. Therefore, $Z = 0 - jX_C = X_C\angle-90^\circ$.

Admittance ($Y$): The mathematical inverse of impedance, measuring how easily a circuit allows current to flow. It is measured in Siemens (S). $Y = \frac{1}{Z} = G + jB$, where $G$ is Conductance and $B$ is Susceptance.

3.5 AC Power and Power Factor

In AC circuits containing reactance (inductors or capacitors), the current and voltage waveforms are out of phase. Because $p(t) = v(t) \cdot i(t)$, this phase shift creates different components of power.

  • Active Power ($P$): The true, useful power permanently consumed and dissipated by resistors. Measured in Watts (W). $P = V_{rms} I_{rms} \cos(\Phi)$.
  • Reactive Power ($Q$): The power that continuously oscillates back and forth between the source and reactive elements without doing any useful net work. Measured in Volt-Amperes Reactive (VAR). $Q = V_{rms} I_{rms} \sin(\Phi)$.
  • Apparent Power ($S$): The total absolute power capacity the source must provide (the vector sum of Active and Reactive power). Measured in Volt-Amperes (VA). $S = V_{rms} I_{rms}$ or $S = \sqrt{P^2 + Q^2}$.

The Impedance & Power Triangles (Inductive Load)

Power Factor (pf)

The Power Factor is a critical metric of electrical efficiency, defined as the ratio of true Active Power to Apparent Power.

$$pf = \frac{P}{S} = \cos(\Phi)$$

Where $\Phi$ is the phase angle difference between the applied voltage and the resulting current. A power factor of 1 (unity) means the circuit is purely resistive and 100% efficient. A power factor of 0 means the circuit is purely reactive, drawing heavy current but producing zero useful work.
