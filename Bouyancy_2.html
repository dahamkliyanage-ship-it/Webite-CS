4.0 Buoyancy and Stability

Understanding Archimedes' Principle, the forces acting on submerged and floating bodies, metacentric height, and the critical conditions for the stability of vessels.

4.1 Introduction to Buoyancy

A fluid exerts a resultant upward force on any object submerged within it. This upward force due to a fluid in equilibrium is known as the buoyancy or upthrust ($U$). Determining buoyancy is vital in the engineering design of ships, boats, buoys, and underwater structures.

Archimedes' Principle: The buoyancy has a magnitude exactly equal to the weight of the displaced volume of fluid, and it acts vertically upwards through the centroid of the displaced volume (known as the Center of Buoyancy).

When an object is submerged in a fluid, an equal volume of fluid is displaced. The surrounding fluid exerts the identical force on the submerged object as it would have exerted on the displaced volume of fluid to keep it in equilibrium.

Characteristics of Buoyancy ($U$):

  • Magnitude: $U = V \rho g = V \gamma$ (where $V$ is the submerged displaced volume).
  • Direction: Acts vertically upwards.
  • Line of Action: Passes through the Center of Buoyancy ($B$), which is the exact geometric centroid of the displaced fluid volume.

Buoyancy on a Submerged Body

Important Distinction:

  • The position of $B$ (Center of Buoyancy) depends only on the geometric shape of the displaced fluid volume.
  • The position of $G$ (Center of Gravity) depends on the weight distribution inside the solid body.
  • If the body is of perfectly uniform density, $B$ and $G$ will coincide.

4.2 Equilibrium of Submerged Bodies

The forces acting on a fully submerged body are its Weight ($W$) acting downwards through $G$, and the Buoyancy ($U$) acting upwards through $B$.

  • If $U = W$, the body remains in perfectly neutral vertical equilibrium exactly where it is.
  • If $U > W$ (the body is lighter than the displaced fluid), the body will accelerate upwards until it reaches the free surface and floats. At that point, it will displace less fluid until a new equilibrium $U_{new} = W$ is reached.
  • If $U < W$ (the body is denser than the fluid), the body will sink to the bottom.

Floating Bodies

For a floating body to be in vertical equilibrium, the buoyancy $U$ on the submerged volume must be exactly equal to the total weight $W$ of the entire body. Furthermore, $B$ and $G$ must lie on the exact same vertical line to prevent rotation or listing.

4.3 Stability of Submerged and Floating Bodies

Stability determines what happens when a body is given a small angular displacement (tilt) from its equilibrium position.

  • Stable Equilibrium: If displaced, forces/moments act to return the body to its original position.
  • Unstable Equilibrium: If displaced, forces/moments act to move the body further away from its original position.
  • Neutral Equilibrium: If displaced, the body remains at rest in the new displaced position.

4.3.1 Stability of Fully Submerged Bodies

For fully submerged bodies (like submarines or weather balloons), the displaced volume does not change shape when the body rotates. Therefore, the Center of Buoyancy ($B$) remains completely fixed relative to the body.

Rule for Fully Submerged Bodies: Stability depends entirely on the relative vertical positions of $B$ and $G$.

  • Stable: $B$ is above $G$. (A tilt creates a restoring couple).
  • Unstable: $B$ is below $G$. (A tilt creates an overturning couple).
  • Neutral: $B$ and $G$ coincide.

4.3.2 Stability of Floating Bodies

Floating bodies are more complex. When a floating body tilts (e.g., a ship rolling), the shape of the submerged volume changes. Consequently, the Center of Buoyancy ($B$) shifts to a new position ($B'$).

The Metacenter ($M$): The vertical line of action of the new buoyant force acting upwards through $B'$ intersects the original vertical axis of the ship at a point called the Metacenter ($M$).

The distance between the Center of Gravity ($G$) and the Metacenter ($M$) is the Metacentric Height ($GM$). This is the ultimate measure of stability for ships and floating vessels.

Stability of a Floating Body (Ship Roll)

Rule for Floating Bodies: Stability depends on the relative vertical positions of $M$ and $G$.

  • Stable: $M$ is above $G$ ($GM > 0$). The upward force at $B'$ and downward force at $G$ create a restoring moment.
  • Unstable: $M$ is below $G$ ($GM < 0$). The couple creates an overturning moment, causing the vessel to capsize.
  • Neutral: $M$ coincides with $G$ ($GM = 0$).

The Restoring Moment is given by: $M_R = W \cdot (GM) \sin\theta$. Hence, $GM$ is a direct measure of the stability of a floating body.

4.4 Determination of Metacentric Height ($GM$)

1. Theoretical Value

If the exact geometry of the submerged volume is known, the metacentric height can be calculated analytically.

The distance from the original Center of Buoyancy ($B$) to the Metacenter ($M$) is called the Metacentric Radius ($BM$).

$$BM = \frac{I}{V}$$

Where:

  • $I =$ Second moment of area of the waterline plane (the cross-section of the ship cut by the free surface) about its centroidal axis of rotation.
  • $V =$ Total submerged volume of the fluid displaced.

Since $M$, $G$, and $B$ lie on the same vertical axis in the equilibrium position, the Metacentric Height ($GM$) is found by geometry:

$$GM = BM - BG$$

$$GM = \frac{I}{V} - BG$$

Where $BG$ is the vertical distance between the Center of Gravity and the original Center of Buoyancy.

2. Time Period of Oscillation

When a stable floating body undergoes a small angular displacement, it oscillates. The time period ($T$) of this simple harmonic motion is directly related to the metacentric height:

$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{k^2}{g \cdot GM}}$$

Where $k$ is the radius of gyration of the body. Note that a very large $GM$ provides great stability but results in a very short time period (a "stiff" and uncomfortable, snappy ship roll).

4.5 Effect of Liquid Cargo on Stability

The stability of a floating vessel carrying liquid with a free surface (e.g., an oil tanker) is severely and adversely affected by the movement of the liquid cargo when the vessel rolls. This phenomenon is known as the Free Surface Effect.

When the vessel tilts, the liquid cargo shifts to the lower side. This shifts the center of gravity of the ship in the same direction as the tilt, creating an overturning moment that reduces the effective metacentric height.

$$Reduction\ of\ Metacentric\ Height\ = \frac{\rho_{cargo}}{\rho_{fluid}} \times \frac{i}{V}$$

$$Effective\ GM = GM - \left( \frac{\rho_c}{\rho_f} \frac{i}{V} \right)$$

Where $i$ is the second moment of area of the free surface of the liquid cargo about the axis of rotation, and $V$ is the total displaced volume of the ship.

Mitigation: Because the reduction is proportional to $i$ (which scales with the cube of the compartment width, $b^3/12$), stability can be massively improved by dividing large liquid compartments into several smaller longitudinal compartments. Dividing a tank into $n$ equal compartments reduces the stability loss by a factor of $1/n^2$.
