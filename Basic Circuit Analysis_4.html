2.0 Basic Circuit Analysis

Understand the foundational properties of electric circuits, including current, voltage, resistance, basic circuit elements, and Kirchhoff's Laws.

2.1 Electric Current & Voltage

Electric Current ($i$ or $I$): Current is the rate of flow of electric charge past a point or region. By convention, current flows from a more positive potential to a more negative potential. This is known as conventional current. However, the actual physical charge carriers (electrons) flow in the exact opposite direction. Time-invariant current (Direct Current or DC) is usually denoted by $I$, while time-varying current (Alternating Current or AC) is denoted by $i$.

$$i = \frac{dq}{dt}$$

$$q = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} i \cdot dt$$

Conventional Current vs. Electron Flow

Voltage ($v$ or $V$): Force is required to move a charge against an electric field. The potential difference (or voltage) is defined as the work ($W$) required to move a unit charge from one point to another. One Volt is the potential difference between two points when one Joule of energy is used to move one Coulomb of charge.

$$v = \frac{dW}{dq}$$

Power ($p$ or $P$): Power is the rate at which work is done (Joules per second, or Watts).

$$p = \frac{dW}{dt} = \frac{dW}{dq} \cdot \frac{dq}{dt} = v \cdot i$$

$$Energy (W) = \int p \cdot dt = \int v \cdot i \cdot dt$$

Sign Convention: If current enters the positive terminal of an element, power is being absorbed (positive). If current leaves the positive terminal, power is being supplied (negative).

2.2 Circuit Elements and Sources

Electrical circuits consist of active and passive elements. Active elements (like batteries, independent voltage/current sources, or dependent sources) are capable of generating electrical energy. Passive elements cannot generate electrical energy, but they can consume, dissipate, or store it.

Passive Circuit Elements:

  • Resistor (R): Dissipates energy purely as heat. Resistance restricts current flow due to the scattering of electrons through collisions with atoms. Ohm's Law dictates the V-I relationship: $v = i \cdot R$. The unit is the Ohm ($\Omega$). Power dissipated: $p = i^2R = \frac{v^2}{R}$.
  • Capacitor (C): Stores energy in an electric field established between two conducting plates separated by an insulating dielectric. Charge $q = Cv$, and the current-voltage relationship is $i = C \frac{dv}{dt}$. Energy stored: $W = \frac{1}{2}Cv^2$. Capacitors act as an open circuit to steady DC.
  • Inductor (L): Stores energy in a magnetic field when current flows through a coil of wire. Voltage is induced proportional to the rate of change of current: $v = L \frac{di}{dt}$. Energy stored: $W = \frac{1}{2}Li^2$. Inductors act as a short circuit to steady DC.

2.3 Equivalence Resistance, Capacitance & Inductance

Circuit elements are frequently connected in series or parallel (shunt) configurations. Reducing these networks to a single equivalent component greatly simplifies circuit analysis.

Series vs. Parallel Resistor Configurations

Series Connections (Current is constant)
  • Resistors: $R_{eq} = R_1 + R_2 + ... + R_n$
  • Inductors: $L_{eq} = L_1 + L_2 + ... + L_n$
  • Capacitors: $\frac{1}{C_{eq}} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + ... + \frac{1}{C_n}$
Parallel / Shunt Connections (Voltage is constant)
  • Resistors: $\frac{1}{R_{eq}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + ... + \frac{1}{R_n}$
  • Inductors: $\frac{1}{L_{eq}} = \frac{1}{L_1} + \frac{1}{L_2} + ... + \frac{1}{L_n}$
  • Capacitors: $C_{eq} = C_1 + C_2 + ... + C_n$

2.4 Circuit Analysis: Kirchhoff's Laws

Gustav Kirchhoff developed two fundamental laws based on the conservation of charge and energy. These laws form the absolute basis for analyzing any lumped-parameter electrical circuit.

Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

Based on the principle of conservation of charge. It states that the algebraic sum of all currents entering and leaving a node is absolutely zero. Alternatively, the sum of currents entering a node equals the sum of currents leaving the node.

$$\sum_{n=1}^{N} i_n = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \sum i_{in} = \sum i_{out}$$

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

Based on the principle of conservation of energy. It states that the algebraic sum of voltages around any closed path (loop) in an electrical circuit equals zero. Energy supplied equals energy dropped.

$$\sum_{m=1}^{M} v_m = 0$$

Visualizing KCL and KVL

Division Principles

Voltage Division Principle (Series Circuits): Used to rapidly find the voltage across a specific resistor in a series circuit without finding the total current first. The voltage drops proportionately to the resistance.

$$v_x = \frac{R_x}{R_{eq}} \cdot V_{total}$$

Current Division Principle (Parallel Circuits): Used to find the current flowing through a specific branch in a parallel circuit. The current takes the path of least resistance, so it is inversely proportional to the branch resistance.

For two parallel branches: $$i_1 = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} \cdot I_{total}$$
