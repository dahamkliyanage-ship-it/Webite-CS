Introduction to Improper Integrals

The Riemann integral is only defined for a bounded function on a compact interval (or a finite union of such intervals). Nevertheless, we frequently want to integrate an unbounded function or a function on an infinite interval. One way to interpret such an integral is as a limit of Riemann integrals; this limit is called an improper Riemann integral.

Functions Unbounded at an Endpoint

Definition:

Suppose that $f : (a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is integrable on $[c,b]$ for every $a < c < b$. Then the improper integral of $f$ on $[a,b]$ is:
$$ \int_a^b f = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0^+} \int_{a+\epsilon}^b f. $$
The improper integral converges if this limit exists (as a finite real number), otherwise it diverges.

Similarly, if $f : [a,b) \to \mathbb{R}$ is integrable on $[a,c]$ for every $a < c < b$, then:
$$ \int_a^b f = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0^+} \int_a^{b-\epsilon} f. $$
Example: The p-test for bounded intervals

If $p > 0$, the integral $\int_0^1 \frac{1}{x^p} dx$ isn't defined as a Riemann integral since $\frac{1}{x^p}$ is unbounded on $(0, 1]$. The corresponding improper integral is:
$$ \int_0^1 \frac{1}{x^p} dx = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0^+} \int_\epsilon^1 \frac{1}{x^p} dx. $$
For $p \neq 1$, we have:
$$ \int_\epsilon^1 \frac{1}{x^p} dx = \frac{1 - \epsilon^{1-p}}{1 - p}. $$
So the improper integral converges if $0 < p < 1$, with limit $\frac{1}{1-p}$, and diverges to $\infty$ if $p > 1$. Also, the integral diverges (more slowly) to $\infty$ if $p = 1$ since $\int_\epsilon^1 \frac{1}{x} dx = -\ln \epsilon$.

Integrals on Unbounded Intervals

We define improper integrals on an unbounded interval as limits of integrals on bounded intervals.

Definition:

Suppose that $f : [a, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}$ is integrable on $[a,r]$ for every $r > a$. Then the improper integral of $f$ on $[a, \infty)$ is:
$$ \int_a^\infty f = \lim_{r \to \infty} \int_a^r f. $$
Similarly, if $f : (-\infty, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is integrable on $[r,b]$ for every $r < b$, then:
$$ \int_{-\infty}^b f = \lim_{r \to -\infty} \int_r^b f. $$
If $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is integrable on every compact interval, then:
$$ \int_{-\infty}^\infty f = \lim_{s \to \infty} \int_{-s}^c f + \lim_{r \to \infty} \int_c^r f, $$
where we split the integral at an arbitrary point $c \in \mathbb{R}$. Note that each limit is required to exist separately.
Example: The p-test for infinite intervals

Suppose $p > 0$. The improper integral:
$$ \int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^p} dx = \lim_{r \to \infty} \int_1^r \frac{1}{x^p} dx = \lim_{r \to \infty} \frac{r^{1-p} - 1}{1 - p} $$
converges to $1/(p-1)$ if $p > 1$ and diverges to $\infty$ if $0 < p < 1$. It also diverges (more slowly) if $p = 1$ since $\int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x} dx = \lim_{r \to \infty} \ln r = \infty$.

Thus, we get a convergent improper integral if the integrand decays sufficiently rapidly as $x \to \infty$ (faster than $1/x$).

Absolute and Conditional Convergence

The convergence of improper integrals is analogous to the convergence of series.

Definition:

An improper integral $\int_a^b f$ is absolutely convergent if the improper integral $\int_a^b |f|$ converges, and conditionally convergent if $\int_a^b f$ converges but $\int_a^b |f|$ diverges.
Theorem:

If $\int_a^\infty f dx$ is absolutely convergent, then the improper integral is convergent. i.e. $\int_a^\infty |f|dx < \infty \implies \int_a^\infty f dx < \infty$.
Proof:
By definition we know $-|f(x)| \le f(x) \le |f(x)|$. Now as:
$$ 0 \le f(x) + |f(x)| \le 2|f(x)| $$
It is given that $\int_a^\infty |f(x)| dx < \infty$. Therefore, by the Comparison Theorem $\int_a^\infty (f(x) + |f(x)|)dx < \infty$. Hence:
$$ \int_a^\infty f(x)dx = \int_a^\infty (f(x) + |f(x)|)dx - \int_a^\infty |f(x)|dx < \infty. $$

Comparison Theorems

Theorem(Comparison Theorem):

Suppose that $f, g$ are two continuous functions for $x \ge a$ such that $0 \le g(x) \le f(x)$. Then:
  • $\int_a^\infty f dx$ converges $\implies \int_a^\infty g dx$ also converges.
  • $\int_a^\infty g dx$ diverges $\implies \int_a^\infty f dx$ also diverges.
Proof Outline: Let $G(t) = \int_a^t g(x)dx \le \int_a^t f(x)dx$ for $x \ge a$. Since $g$ is non-negative, $G(t)$ is non-decreasing. If $\int f$ converges, $G(t)$ is bounded above, so its limit as $t \to \infty$ exists (it converges). If $\int g$ diverges to $\infty$, since $\int f \ge \int g$, it forces $\int f$ to also diverge to $\infty$.
Theorem(Limit Comparison Theorem):

Let $f$ and $g$ be continuous positive functions $\forall x$ and $\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = k$. Then:
  • $0 < k < \infty \implies \int_a^\infty g dx$ converges $\iff \int_a^\infty f dx$ converges.
  • $k = 0 \implies \int_a^\infty g dx$ converges $\implies \int_a^\infty f dx$ converges.
  • $k = \infty \implies \int_a^\infty f dx$ converges $\implies \int_a^\infty g dx$ converges.

Added Worked Examples

Example A: Evaluating an Improper Integral

Evaluate the integral $\int_0^\infty x e^{-x} dx$.

Step 1: Set up the limit.
$$ \lim_{r \to \infty} \int_0^r x e^{-x} dx $$
Step 2: Integrate by parts. Let $u = x, dv = e^{-x}dx$, so $du = dx, v = -e^{-x}$.
$$ = \lim_{r \to \infty} \left[ -x e^{-x} \right]_0^r - \int_0^r -e^{-x} dx $$ $$ = \lim_{r \to \infty} \left( -r e^{-r} + 0 \right) + \left[ -e^{-x} \right]_0^r $$ $$ = \lim_{r \to \infty} \left( \frac{-r}{e^r} - e^{-r} + 1 \right) $$
Step 3: Evaluate the limit. Using L'Hôpital's rule for $\lim_{r \to \infty} \frac{r}{e^r} = \lim_{r \to \infty} \frac{1}{e^r} = 0$.
$$ = 0 - 0 + 1 = 1 $$
The integral converges to $1$.
Example B: Using the Comparison Theorem

Determine whether $\int_1^\infty \frac{\sin^2(x)}{x^2} dx$ converges or diverges.

Solution: We know that $0 \le \sin^2(x) \le 1$ for all real $x$. Therefore:
$$ 0 \le \frac{\sin^2(x)}{x^2} \le \frac{1}{x^2} $$
By the p-test (Example), we know that $\int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^2} dx$ converges because $p = 2 > 1$.
Since our function is bounded above by a convergent function, by the Comparison Theorem (Theorem 0.6.2), the integral $\int_1^\infty \frac{\sin^2(x)}{x^2} dx$ also converges.
