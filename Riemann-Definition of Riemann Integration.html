Partitions

Two intervals are said to be almost disjoint if they are disjoint or intersect only at a common endpoint. For example, the intervals $[1, 3]$ and $[3, 4]$ are almost disjoint, whereas $[0, 4]$ and $[2, 5]$ are not.

Definition(Partition):

Let $I$ be a nonempty, compact interval (closed and bounded). A partition of $I$ is a finite collection $\{I_1, I_2, \dots, I_n\}$ of almost disjoint, nonempty, compact sub intervals whose union is $I$.

A partition $P$ of $[a, b]$ with subintervals $I_k = [x_{k-1}, x_k]$ is determined by the set of endpoints of the intervals $a = x_0 < x_1 < x_2 < \dots < x_{n-1} < x_n = b$.

We will denote a partition $P$ either by its intervals $P = \{I_1, I_2, \dots, I_n\}$ or by the set of endpoints of the intervals $P = \{x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}$. There is always one more endpoint than interval.
Example:

The set of intervals $\{[0, 1/6], [1/6, 1/5], [1/5, 1/4], [1/4, 1/3], [1/3, 1/2], [1/2, 1]\}$ is a partition of $[0, 1]$. The corresponding set of endpoints are $\{0, 1/6, 1/5, 1/4, 1/3, 1/2, 1\}$.

We denote the length of an interval $I = [a, b]$ by $|I| = b - a$. For the interval $I$, the sum of the lengths $|I_k| = x_k - x_{k-1}$ of the almost disjoint sub-intervals in a partition is equal to the length of the whole interval. This is geometrically obvious; algebraically, it follows from the telescoping series:
$$ \sum_{k=1}^n |I_k| = \sum_{k=1}^n (x_k - x_{k-1}) $$ $$ = x_n - x_{n-1} + x_{n-1} - x_{n-2} + \dots + x_2 - x_1 + x_1 - x_0 $$ $$ = x_n - x_0 = |I|. $$

Upper and Lower Riemann Sums

Suppose that $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is a bounded function on the compact interval $I = [a, b]$ with:
$$ M = \sup_I f, \quad m = \inf_I f. $$
If $P = \{I_1, I_2, \dots, I_n\}$ is a partition of $I$, let
$$ M_k = \sup_{I_k} f = \sup \{f(x) : x \in [x_{k-1}, x_k]\}, \quad m_k = \inf_{I_k} f = \inf \{f(x) : x \in [x_{k-1}, x_k]\}. $$
Upper Riemann Sum
The upper Riemann sum of $f$ with respect to the partition $P$ is defined by:
$$ U(f; P) = \sum_{k=1}^n M_k|I_k| = \sum_{k=1}^n M_k(x_k - x_{k-1}) $$
Geometrically, $U(f; P)$ is the sum of the areas of rectangles based on the intervals $I_k$ that lie above the graph of $f$.

Lower Riemann Sum
The lower Riemann sum of $f$ with respect to the partition $P$ is defined by:
$$ L(f; P) = \sum_{k=1}^n m_k|I_k| = \sum_{k=1}^n m_k(x_k - x_{k-1}). $$
Geometrically, $L(f; P)$ is the sum of the areas of rectangles that lie below the graph of $f$.

For all $k$, $m_k = \inf\{f(x)\} \le M_k = \sup\{f(x)\}$. This leads directly to our first lemma.
Lemma:
Let $f$ be a bounded function on $[a,b]$ and let $P$ be any partition of $[a,b]$. Then
$$ L(f, P) \le U(f, P). $$
Refinements of partitions
If $P$ and $Q$ are partitions of $[a, b]$, where $P \subseteq Q$, then $Q$ is called a refinement of $P$. Note: If $P_1$ and $P_2$ are partitions of $[a,b]$, then $Q = P_1 \cup P_2$ is a refinement of both $P_1$ and $P_2$.
Example:
Consider the partitions of $[0, 1]$ with endpoints $P = \{0, 1/2, 1\}$, $Q = \{0, 1/3, 2/3, 1\}$, $R = \{0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1\}$. Thus, $P, Q,$ and $R$ partition $[0, 1]$ into intervals of equal length $1/2, 1/3,$ and $1/4$, respectively. Then $Q$ is not a refinement of $P$ but $R$ is a refinement of $P$.
Lemma:
If $Q$ is a refinement of $P$, then
$$ L(f, P) \le L(f, Q) \le U(f, Q) \le U(f, P) $$
Proof:
From the previous lemma, we already know that $L(f; Q) \le U(f; Q)$. We'll show that $L(f; P) \le L(f; Q)$. (The proof for Upper Sums is similar). Let $P = \{x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n\} \subseteq Q$. Now $\forall k$, let $Q \cap [x_{k-1}, x_k] = \{x_{k-1} = x_{k0}, x_{k1}, \dots, x_{ki_k} = x_k\}$. Then
$$ m_k = \inf\{f(x) | x \in [x_{k-1}, x_k]\} \le m_{ki} = \inf\{f(x) | x \in [x_{k(i-1)}, x_{ki}]\} $$
for $i = 1, 2, \dots, i_k$. Now let,
$$ L(f; P) = \sum_{k=1}^n m_k|I_k| = \sum_{k=1}^n m_k\{|I_{k1}| + |I_{k2}| + \dots + |I_{ki_k}|\} $$ $$ \le \sum_{k=1}^n \{m_{k1}|I_{k1}| + m_{k2}|I_{k2}| + \dots + m_{ki_k}|I_{ki_k}|\} $$ $$ = \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{i=1}^{i_k} m_{ki}|I_{ki}| = L(Q; f). $$
Hence $L(f; P) \le L(f; Q)$.
Lemma:
If $P_1$ and $P_2$ are any two partitions of $[a, b]$, then
$$ L(f, P_1) \le U(f, P_2) $$
Proof: Let $Q = P_1 \cup P_2$ be the common refinement of $P_1$ and $P_2$. By the previous lemmas, $L(f, P_1) \le L(f, Q) \le U(f, Q) \le U(f, P_2)$.

Upper Integrals, Lower Integrals, and Integrability

Let $\mathscr{P}$ be the collection of all possible partitions of the interval $[a, b]$.

Definition(Upper and Lower Integrals):
The upper integral of $f$ is $U(f) := \inf\{U(f, P) : P \in \mathscr{P}\}$.
The lower integral of $f$ is $L(f) := \sup\{L(f, P) : P \in \mathscr{P}\}$.
A commonly used alternative notation uses a bar over/under the integral sign: $\overline{\int_a^b} f$ and $\underline{\int_a^b} f$.

Lemma: For any bounded function $f$ on $[a, b]$, it is always the case that $L(f) \le U(f)$.

Definition:
A bounded function $f : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is Riemann integrable on $[a, b]$ if its upper integral $U(f)$ and lower integral $L(f)$ are equal. In that case, the Riemann integral of $f$ on $[a, b]$, denoted by $\int_a^b f(x)dx$, is defined.

An unbounded function is not Riemann integrable. Riemann integral is defined for $f$ on a closed interval $[a, b]$.

Examples

Example:
A constant function is Riemann integrable on $[a, b]$.
Solution: Let $f(x) = C, \forall x \in [a, b]$ where $C \in \mathbb{R}$ is a constant. Clearly $f$ is bounded and $\inf f = \sup f = C$. Let $P = \{x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ be a partition. Let $m_k, M_k$ be inf and sup of $f$ on $I_k$. Since $f(x) = C$,
$$ L(f, P) = \sum m_k|I_k| = C\sum |I_k| = C(b - a) $$ $$ U(f, P) = \sum M_k|I_k| = C\sum |I_k| = C(b - a) $$
Therefore, $\overline{\int_a^b} f = \inf U(f, P) = \sup L(f, P) = \underline{\int_a^b} f = C(b - a)$. $f$ is Riemann integrable and $\int_a^b C dx = C(b - a)$.
Example:
Define $f : [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x) = 1/x$ if $0 < x \le 1$, and $0$ if $x = 0$. Then $\int_0^1 \frac{1}{x} dx$ isn't defined as a Riemann integral because $f$ is unbounded. In fact, if $0 < x_1 < x_2 < \dots < x_{n-1} < 1$ is a partition, then $\sup_{[0, x_1]} f = \infty$, so the upper Riemann sums of $f$ are not well-defined.
Example:
An integral with an unbounded interval of integration, such as $\int_0^\infty x dx$ also isn't defined as a Riemann integral. A partition of $[1, \infty)$ into finitely many intervals contains at least one unbounded interval.
Example:
$f(x) = 0$ if $0 < x \le 1$ and $1$ if $x = 0$ is Riemann integrable, and $\int_0^1 f dx = 0$.
To show this, let $P = I_1, I_2, \dots, I_n$ be a partition of $[0, 1]$. Then, since $f(x) = 0$ for $x > 0$,
$$ M_k = \sup_{I_k} f = 0, \quad m_k = \inf_{I_k} f = 0 \quad \text{for } k = 2, 3, \dots n $$
The first interval is $I_1 = [0, x_1]$. Since $f(0)=1$ and $f(x)=0$ for $0 < x \le x_1$, $M_1 = 1, m_1 = 0$. It follows that $U(f, P) = x_1$ and $L(f, P) = 0$. Thus, $L(f) = 0$ and $U(f) = \inf\{x_1 : 0 < x_1 \le 1\} = 0$. Since they are equal, the integral of $f$ is 0. We need to show that a particular function is integrable before actually integrating it.
Added Worked Example: Calculating a specific Riemann Sum
Consider $f(x) = x^2$ on the interval $[0, 2]$. Let's evaluate $U(f; P)$ and $L(f; P)$ using the uniform partition $P = \{0, 1, 2\}$.
The subintervals are $I_1 = [0, 1]$ and $I_2 = [1, 2]$. The lengths are $|I_1| = 1$ and $|I_2| = 1$.
* For $I_1$: $m_1 = \inf_{[0,1]} (x^2) = 0$ and $M_1 = \sup_{[0,1]} (x^2) = 1$.
* For $I_2$: $m_2 = \inf_{[1,2]} (x^2) = 1$ and $M_2 = \sup_{[1,2]} (x^2) = 4$.
Lower Sum $L(f; P) = m_1(1) + m_2(1) = 0(1) + 1(1) = 1$.
Upper Sum $U(f; P) = M_1(1) + M_2(1) = 1(1) + 4(1) = 5$.
