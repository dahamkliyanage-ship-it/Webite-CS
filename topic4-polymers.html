Topic 04

Degradation of Polymers & Ceramics

Polymer Degradation

Unlike metal corrosion (which is strictly electrochemical), polymer degradation is physiochemical in nature. It is a change in the physical or chemical structure of the polymer chain under the influence of environmental factors (heat, UV light, chemicals, mechanical stress).

The susceptibility of a polymer depends heavily on its structure. For example, chains containing aromatic rings are highly susceptible to UV degradation, while hydrocarbon-based polymers are highly susceptible to thermal degradation.

Mechanisms of Polymer Failure

  • Chain Scission: Environmental factors cause the primary molecular bonds to rupture. This severs the chains, leading directly to a massive decrease in the molecular weight and strength of the polymer.
  • Swelling: When exposed to liquids, the liquid diffuses and absorbs into the polymer. The macromolecules are forced apart (the specimen expands). This increase in separation drastically reduces the secondary intermolecular bonding forces, causing the material to become weak and soft.
  • Dissolution: This is simply the continuation of severe swelling until the polymer becomes completely soluble and breaks apart.

Note: While degradation is undesirable during use, it becomes highly desirable after use in the form of Biodegradation.

Corrosion of Ceramics

In contrast to metals and polymers, ceramic materials are exceptionally resistant to corrosion. Because their primary atomic bonds are already fully stable, they do not easily react with external environments. This extreme resilience is why glass is ubiquitously used to contain harsh liquids, and refractory ceramics are used to withstand incredibly high temperatures and provide thermal insulation.
