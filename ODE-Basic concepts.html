Basic Concepts

Definition(Differential Equation)

Equations which are composed of an unknown function and its derivatives are called differential equations.

Differential equations are categorized into two main classes:

Definition(ODE and PDE)

  • When a differential equation involves one independent variable, and one or more dependent variables, the equation is called an ordinary differential equation (ODE). In other words, it contains only ordinary derivatives.
  • Partial differential equation (PDE) involves two or more independent variables, and one or more dependent variables. In other words, it contains partial derivatives.
Example 1.1: ODEs vs PDEs

The following are examples of ordinary differential equations, which frequently appear in first-semester engineering tasks like RLC circuit analysis:
$$ L\frac{d^2i}{dt^2} + R\frac{di}{dt} + \frac{1}{C}i = 0 \quad \text{(RLC Circuit)} $$ $$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \cos(x) $$ $$ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + 8y = e^{-2x} $$ $$ \frac{dy}{dx} \frac{d^3y}{dx^3} - 4 \left( \frac{dy}{dx} \right)^2 = 2 $$
The following are examples of partial differential equations:
$$ \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 0 $$ $$ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} $$
Note: PDEs have important engineering applications, such as calculating the deflection of elastic beams under complex continuous loads, and are mathematically more complicated than ODEs.

Linear vs. Nonlinear Differential Equations

Definition(Linear and Nonlinear)

  • A linear differential equation is a differential equation that is defined by a linear polynomial in the unknown function (dependent variable) and its derivatives. It takes the form:
    $$ P_0(x)y + P_1(x)y' + P_2(x)y'' + \dots + P_n(x)y^{(n)} + Q(x) = 0 $$
    Where $P_0(x), \dots, P_n(x)$, and $Q(x)$ are either zero, constant, or depend only on $x$, not on $y$. They are differentiable functions that do not need to be linear. $y', \dots, y^{(n)}$ are successive derivatives of the unknown function $y$ of the independent variable $x$.
  • Nonlinear differential equations are any equations that cannot be written in the above form. In particular, these include all equations containing $y, y', y''$, etc., raised to any power such as $y^2, y'^3$, nonlinear functions of $y$ or any derivative to any order (such as $\sin(y)$ or $e^{ty}$), or any product or function of these.

Order and Degree

Definition:

The order of a differential equation is determined by the highest order derivative which occurs in it after the equation has been rationalized, i.e., made free from radicals and fractions so far as derivatives are concerned (polynomial form).

The degree of a differential equation is determined by the exponent of the highest derivative in it.
Further Example: Finding Order and Degree

Consider the differential equation:
$$ \left( \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} \right)^3 + 4\frac{dy}{dx} - y = 0 $$
  • The Order is $2$, because the highest derivative present is the second derivative $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$.
  • The Degree is $3$, because the exponent attached to that highest derivative is $3$.

Standard Form and Solutions

In this module we mainly discuss first and second order differential equations and their solutions.

Definition(Standard Form)

A differential equation is said to be in standard form if we can solve for $\frac{dy}{dx}$, i.e.,
$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y) $$
We will often write a given equation in standard form so that we can identify the form of the function $f(x, y)$.
Definition(Solution, ODE)

A function $y(x) = \phi(x)$ is called a solution of $y' = f(x, y)$, if it satisfies:
$$ \phi'(x) = f(x, \phi(x)) $$
To verify that a function $y = f(x)$ is a solution of the ODE, we substitute the function into both sides of the differential equation.
Example 1.3

A solution of
$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = x^2 $$
is
$$ y = \frac{x^3}{3} $$
We use the expression "a solution" rather than "the solution" because solutions are not unique. For example:
$$ y = \frac{x^3}{3} + 1 $$
is also a solution of $y' = x^2$. We say the solution is not unique.

If we integrate both sides of $y' = x^2$:
$$ \int dy = \int x^2 \, dx $$ $$ y = \frac{x^3}{3} + C $$
where $C$ is any arbitrary constant.

The solution $y = \frac{x^3}{3} + C$ with the arbitrary constant is called the general solution of the differential equation. A constant like $C$ that is allowed to take on multiple values in an equation is sometimes called a parameter. Sometimes we will say $y = \frac{x^3}{3} + C$ represents the one-parameter family of solutions, integral curves or solution curves of the differential equation, with parameter $C$.

Initial Value Problems (IVP)

Definition(Initial Value Problem (IVP))

An initial value problem is given by:
$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y) $$ $$ y(x_0) = y_0 $$
where $(x_0, y_0)$ be a point in the domain of $f(x, y)$. The equation $y(x_0) = y_0$ is called an initial condition for the initial value problem.
Definition(Solution, IVP)

The function $y(x)$ is called a solution of the initial value problem:
$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y) $$ $$ y(x_0) = y_0 $$
if $y(x)$ satisfies both the ODE and the Initial Condition (IC).
Theorem(Picard's Existence and Uniqueness Theorem)

Consider the Initial Value Problem:
$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y) $$ $$ y(x_0) = y_0 $$
Suppose $f(x, y)$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ are continuous functions in some open neighbourhood $D \in \mathbf{R}^2$ which contains the point $(x_0, y_0)$. Then the IVP has a unique solution in some closed interval which contains $x_0$.

Detail: Picard's Theorem is a foundational mathematical guarantee. It assures us that as long as our differential equation's function and its partial derivative with respect to $y$ are reasonably smooth (continuous) around our starting point, a solution will not only exist but will be the only solution through that specific point.
