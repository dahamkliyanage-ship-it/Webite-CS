Vector Equation of a Plane

A plane in three-dimensional space can be uniquely determined in several ways. We will explore the three most common mathematical formulations: using a point and two parallel vectors, using a point and a perpendicular (normal) vector, and using three non-collinear points.

The plane that contains a point with the position vector $\underline{r_0}$ and is parallel to two vectors $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$

Parametric Vector Equation

Suppose $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$ are two vectors in 3-space so that $\underline{a} \times \underline{b} \neq \underline{0}$ (meaning they are not parallel to each other) and $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ is a point on the plane with position vector $\underline{r_0}$.

Let $(x, y, z)$ be an arbitrary point on the plane with the position vector $\underline{r}$.
Then the vector connecting these two points, $\underline{r} - \underline{r_0}$, must lie entirely on the plane.

Because $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$ determine the "grid" or direction of the plane, any vector on the plane can be expressed as a linear combination of $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$. Then $\underline{r} - \underline{r_0} = s\underline{a} + t\underline{b}$ for some scalars $s$ and $t$. Therefore,
$$ \underline{r} = \underline{r_0} + s\underline{a} + t\underline{b} \quad \text{where } s, t \in \mathbb{R} $$

Figure 4: A plane defined by position vector $\underline{r_0}$ and direction vectors $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$.
Scalar Triple Product Form

Alternatively, since the vector $(\underline{r} - \underline{r_0})$ lies on the plane formed by vectors $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$, the three vectors are coplanar. As established in the Scalar Triple Product chapter, the volume of a parallelepiped formed by three coplanar vectors is zero.

The equation can be efficiently written as:
$$ (\underline{a} \times \underline{b}) \cdot (\underline{r} - \underline{r_0}) = 0 \quad \text{i.e. } [\underline{r} - \underline{r_0}, \underline{a}, \underline{b}] = 0 $$
Example 1: Parameterizing a Plane

Find the vector equation of the plane passing through the point $P(1, 2, 3)$ and parallel to the vectors $\underline{a} = \underline{i} - \underline{k}$ and $\underline{b} = \underline{j} + \underline{k}$.

We identify the position vector of the point as $\underline{r_0} = \underline{i} + 2\underline{j} + 3\underline{k}$.
Plugging directly into the formula $\underline{r} = \underline{r_0} + s\underline{a} + t\underline{b}$:
$$ \underline{r} = (\underline{i} + 2\underline{j} + 3\underline{k}) + s(\underline{i} - \underline{k}) + t(\underline{j} + \underline{k}) $$
If required, we can expand this into parametric coordinates:
$x = 1 + s$
$y = 2 + t$
$z = 3 - s + t$

The equation of the plane when $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ on the plane and $\underline{n}$ is a normal vector

Normal Vector Equation

Let $(x, y, z)$ be an arbitrary point with the position vector $\underline{r}$. Then the vector $\underline{r} - \underline{r_0}$ lies on the plane.

A normal vector $\underline{n}$ is defined as a vector that is perfectly perpendicular (orthogonal) to the entire plane. Therefore, it must be perpendicular to *every* vector lying on that plane.

Observe that $\underline{r} - \underline{r_0}$ is perpendicular to the vector $\underline{n}$. Therefore, their dot product must be exactly zero:
$$ (\underline{r} - \underline{r_0}) \cdot \underline{n} = 0 $$

Figure 5: Equation of a plane derived using a point and a perpendicular normal vector $\underline{n}$.
Cartesian (Scalar) Equation of the Plane

If we express the normal vector in its components $\underline{n} = a\underline{i} + b\underline{j} + c\underline{k}$, and substitute our position vectors $\underline{r} = \langle x,y,z \rangle$ and $\underline{r_0} = \langle x_0, y_0, z_0 \rangle$, the dot product becomes:
$$ a(x - x_0) + b(y - y_0) + c(z - z_0) = 0 $$
By expanding this and moving the constants to the right side, we obtain the standard Cartesian form of a plane:
$$ ax + by + cz = d \quad \text{where } d = ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0 $$
Note: The coefficients of $x, y, z$ in the Cartesian equation $(a, b, c)$ instantly give you the components of the normal vector!
Example 2: Finding the Cartesian Equation

Find the Cartesian equation of the plane passing through the point $P(2, -1, 4)$ with a normal vector $\underline{n} = 3\underline{i} + 5\underline{j} - 2\underline{k}$.

Using the formula $a(x - x_0) + b(y - y_0) + c(z - z_0) = 0$, we plug in our point coordinates for $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ and normal components for $(a, b, c)$:
$$ 3(x - 2) + 5(y - (-1)) - 2(z - 4) = 0 $$ $$ 3x - 6 + 5y + 5 - 2z + 8 = 0 $$ $$ 3x + 5y - 2z + 7 = 0 $$
Thus, the simplified Cartesian equation is:
$$ 3x + 5y - 2z = -7 $$

Three-Point Equation

Let $\underline{r_0}$, $\underline{r_1}$, and $\underline{r_2}$ be given, and $\underline{r}$ be the position vector of an arbitrary point on the plane.

We can form three distinct vectors that lie completely flat on the plane by subtracting the position vectors of the known points:
  • $\underline{r} - \underline{r_1}$ (from known point 1 to arbitrary point)
  • $\underline{r_0} - \underline{r_1}$ (from known point 1 to known point 0)
  • $\underline{r_2} - \underline{r_1}$ (from known point 1 to known point 2)
Because all three of these vectors are confined to the exact same 2D plane in 3D space, they are coplanar. Therefore, by the rules of the scalar triple product, their volume computation must be zero:
$$ (\underline{r} - \underline{r_1}) \cdot ((\underline{r_0} - \underline{r_1}) \times (\underline{r_2} - \underline{r_1})) = 0 $$

Example 3: Creating an Equation from Three Points

Find the Cartesian equation of the plane passing through the points $A(1, 0, 0)$, $B(0, 1, 0)$, and $C(0, 0, 1)$.

Step 1: Construct two direction vectors lying on the plane.
Let's use point $A$ as our anchor ($\underline{r_1}$).
  • $\overrightarrow{AB} = \underline{r_0} - \underline{r_1} = \langle 0-1, 1-0, 0-0 \rangle = \langle -1, 1, 0 \rangle$
  • $\overrightarrow{AC} = \underline{r_2} - \underline{r_1} = \langle 0-1, 0-0, 1-0 \rangle = \langle -1, 0, 1 \rangle$

Step 2: Find the normal vector $\underline{n}$ by crossing the two direction vectors.
$$ \underline{n} = \overrightarrow{AB} \times \overrightarrow{AC} = \begin{vmatrix} \underline{i} & \underline{j} & \underline{k} \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} $$ $$ \underline{n} = \underline{i}(1 - 0) - \underline{j}(-1 - 0) + \underline{k}(0 - (-1)) = \langle 1, 1, 1 \rangle $$

Step 3: Use the Normal Vector Equation (from 3.3.2)
Using $\underline{n} = \langle 1, 1, 1 \rangle$ and our anchor point $A(1, 0, 0)$:
$$ 1(x - 1) + 1(y - 0) + 1(z - 0) = 0 $$ $$ x + y + z = 1 $$
