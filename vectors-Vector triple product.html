Vector Triple Product

Definition: Suppose $\underline{a}$, $\underline{b}$, and $\underline{c}$ are three vectors. Then the vector triple product of $\underline{a}$, $\underline{b}$, and $\underline{c}$ is defined by:
$$ \underline{a} \times (\underline{b} \times \underline{c}) $$

Insight: Unlike the scalar triple product $[\underline{a}, \underline{b}, \underline{c}]$ which outputs a single real number, the vector triple product outputs a new vector. This is because crossing $\underline{b}$ and $\underline{c}$ gives a vector, and crossing $\underline{a}$ with that result gives yet another vector. Furthermore, this resulting vector will always lie entirely within the plane formed by vectors $\underline{b}$ and $\underline{c}$.

Theorem(The Expansion Formulas):

Calculating two cross products manually using large $3 \times 3$ determinants is tedious and prone to algebraic errors. Fortunately, the vector triple product can be expanded into much simpler dot products.

Suppose $\underline{a}$, $\underline{b}$, and $\underline{c}$ are three vectors. Then:
  • Formula 1: $\underline{a} \times (\underline{b} \times \underline{c}) = (\underline{a} \cdot \underline{c})\underline{b} - (\underline{a} \cdot \underline{b})\underline{c}$
  • Formula 2: $(\underline{a} \times \underline{b}) \times \underline{c} = (\underline{a} \cdot \underline{c})\underline{b} - (\underline{b} \cdot \underline{c})\underline{a}$
Memory Trick (The BAC-CAB Rule): For Formula 1, notice the pattern of the letters on the right side. It spells out "BAC minus CAB". This is the most common way engineers and physicists memorize this expansion identity!
Detailed Example: Using the BAC-CAB Rule

Let $\underline{a} = \underline{i} + \underline{j}$, $\underline{b} = \underline{i} - \underline{k}$, and $\underline{c} = \underline{j} + \underline{k}$.
Evaluate the vector triple product $\underline{a} \times (\underline{b} \times \underline{c})$.

Instead of doing two separate cross-product determinants, we will use Theorem 2.4.1:
$\underline{a} \times (\underline{b} \times \underline{c}) = (\underline{a} \cdot \underline{c})\underline{b} - (\underline{a} \cdot \underline{b})\underline{c}$

Step 1: Calculate the dot products (Scalars)
  • $\underline{a} \cdot \underline{c} = (1)(0) + (1)(1) + (0)(1) = 1$
  • $\underline{a} \cdot \underline{b} = (1)(1) + (1)(0) + (0)(-1) = 1$

Step 2: Substitute the scalars back into the BAC-CAB formula
$$ \underline{a} \times (\underline{b} \times \underline{c}) = (1)\underline{b} - (1)\underline{c} $$ $$ = 1(\underline{i} - \underline{k}) - 1(\underline{j} + \underline{k}) $$

Step 3: Simplify the resulting vector
$$ = \underline{i} - \underline{k} - \underline{j} - \underline{k} $$ $$ = \underline{i} - \underline{j} - 2\underline{k} $$
Notice how much faster this is than computing multiple $3 \times 3$ matrices!

Warning on Associativity: The cross product is not associative. As shown by comparing the two formulas in Theorem 2.4.1, shifting the parentheses completely changes the result: $\underline{a} \times (\underline{b} \times \underline{c}) \neq (\underline{a} \times \underline{b}) \times \underline{c}$.
