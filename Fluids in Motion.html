6.0 Fluids in Motion

Understanding the kinematic principles of fluid in motion, classification of flows, spatial and temporal variations, flow patterns, and the distinction between steady, uniform, laminar, and turbulent flows.

6.1 Introduction to Fluids in Motion

  • Fluid: A substance which flows under shear forces.
  • Flow: Motion with relative movement between fluid particles. Continuous deformation (change in shape) takes place.

6.2 Classification of Fluid Flow

1. Based on Fluid Properties (Density $\rho$ and Viscosity $\mu$)

Density Classification:

Incompressible Flow

No significant variation of density ($\frac{\Delta \rho}{\rho} \ll 1$).

Examples: Pipe/channel flow of liquids, typical gas flow in pipes. (Most engineering applications fall here).

Compressible Flow

Significant variation of density occurs during flow.

Example: Pressure surges in pipes (water hammer), high-speed aerodynamics.

Viscosity Classification:

Non-Viscous (Inviscid) Flow

Viscosity $\mu = 0$. Assumed frictionless for theoretical simplifications.

Viscous Flow ($\mu \neq 0$)

Newtonian: $\mu$ is constant (e.g., water, air).

Non-Newtonian: $\mu$ is not constant (e.g., paints, clays, plastics).

2. Based on Variation of Flow Parameters

Parameters describing the flow (Velocity $V$, Pressure $P$, Flow rate) can vary with Time ($t$) (Temporal) or in Space ($x,y,z$) (Spatial).

Velocity Vector: $V = f(t, x, y, z)$

Temporal Variation (With Time)

  • Steady Flow: No variation of flow parameters with time. $V = f(x,y,z)$ only. $\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = 0$.
    Example: Constant flow rate in a pipe or channel.
  • Unsteady Flow: Variation of flow parameters with time. $V = f(t, x,y,z)$. $\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} \neq 0$.
    Example: Flood waves in a river.

Acceleration in Fluid Flow:

Since $V = f(t, x, y, z)$, the total acceleration $a = \frac{dV}{dt}$ is given by the chain rule:

$$a = \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\frac{dz}{dt}$$

Since $u = \frac{dx}{dt}, v = \frac{dy}{dt}, w = \frac{dz}{dt}$:

$$a = \underbrace{\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}}_{\text{Temporal Accel.}} + \underbrace{u\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial V}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}}_{\text{Convective Accel.}}$$

For a Steady Flow, $\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = 0$, so acceleration is purely convective.

Spatial Variation (In Space)

  • Uniform Flow: No spatial variation of flow parameters. $V = \text{constant}$ or $V = f(t)$ only.
    Example: Constant flow rate in a pipe of uniform cross-section.
  • Non-Uniform Flow: Spatial variation of flow parameters exists. $V = f(x,y,z)$ or $V = f(t,x,y,z)$.
    Example: Flow in a pipe of varying diameter (converging/diverging pipe).

3. Based on Dimensionality (1D, 2D, 3D Flow)

In general, fluid flow is 3D $\rightarrow V = f(t, x, y, z)$. However, variations in certain directions are often negligible.

  • 2D Flow: $V = f(t, x, y)$. Example: Flow over a long weir.
  • 1D Flow: $V = f(t, x)$. Example: Pipe flow (analyzed primarily along the streamline direction).

4. Based on the Nature of Movement of Fluid Particles

Laminar Flow (Orderly)

Fluid particles move in smooth, orderly paths (laminae/layers).

Low velocities. Very little mixing between adjacent layers. Viscous effects are significant.

Examples: Groundwater flow, flow of highly viscous oil in small diameter pipes.

Turbulent Flow (Disorderly)

Fluid particles move in highly erratic, chaotic, and unpredictable paths.

High velocities. Extensive mixing between layers. Viscous effects are not significant compared to inertial forces.

Examples: Most civil engineering applications (river flow, standard pipe flow).

Velocity vs Time: Laminar vs Turbulent Flow

Rotation of Fluid Particles:

  • Rotational Flow: Fluid particles rotate about their own mass centers as they flow. Usually due to shear forces (viscosity) acting on the real fluids (e.g., fluid rolling down an incline).
  • Irrotational Flow: Fluid particles do not rotate about their own axes. Characteristic of theoretical, frictionless (inviscid) fluids.

6.3 Fluid Flow Patterns

Visualizing flow patterns is critical for understanding fluid mechanics. We use several distinct types of mathematical and experimental lines.

  • Streamline: An imaginary curve drawn such that it is everywhere tangent to the instantaneous velocity field. Since it is tangent to the velocity vector, no fluid can flow across a streamline. It is a "snapshot" of the flow at a single instant in time.
  • Pathline: The actual physical path traced by a single, specific fluid particle over a period of time. (Like a long-exposure photograph of a glowing particle).
  • Streakline: The locus (connecting line) of all fluid particles that have continuously passed through a specific, fixed injection point over a period of time. (e.g., injecting a continuous stream of dye or smoke into the flow).
  • Timeline: A line connecting adjacent fluid particles that were marked simultaneously at a given instant (e.g., using a pulsed hydrogen bubble wire). It shows how a uniform block of fluid distorts over time (useful for seeing velocity profiles).

Crucial Rule: For a Steady Flow, the velocity field does not change over time. Therefore, Streamlines, Pathlines, and Streaklines are completely identical and lie exactly on top of each other. In an Unsteady Flow, they are all different shapes.
