8.0 Fluid Machinery

Comprehensive overview of devices that convert fluid energy to mechanical energy or vice versa, including the principles of positive displacement and rotodynamic machines, pump performance, and turbines.

8.1 Introduction to Fluid Machinery

Fluid Machine: A device which transfers energy between its operating mechanical parts (moving/rotating) and a fluid. It converts the energy stored by a fluid into mechanical energy, or vice versa.

Classification Based on the Direction of Energy Transfer:

  • Machine $\rightarrow$ Fluid: Energy is added to the fluid. Examples include Pumps (transfer energy to liquids), and Fans, Blowers, or Compressors (transfer energy to gases).
  • Fluid $\rightarrow$ Machine: Energy is extracted from the fluid. Examples include Turbines and Fluid Motors.
  • Fluid as a Medium: The fluid is used strictly to transmit force/energy without continuous flow. Example: Hydraulic Jack ($P = F/A = f/a$).

8.2 Classification Based on Principle of Operation

a. Positive Displacement Machines

Fluid is taken into an enclosed space and forced out by repeated (cyclic) mechanical action.

Examples: Piston (Reciprocating) pumps, Rotary gear pumps.

Often used for transferring highly viscous fluids (oil, fuel) or generating extremely high pressures.

b. Rotodynamic Machines

Energy is continuously exchanged between a flowing fluid and a spinning rotor or impeller.

Rotating Elements: Impeller (in Pumps), Runner (in Turbines), Rotor (in Fans).

Flow directions can be Axial, Radial, or Mixed.

8.3 Centrifugal Pumps (Rotodynamic)

Centrifugal pumps are the most commonly used radial-flow rotodynamic machines. Their primary purpose is to raise a liquid from a lower elevation to a higher elevation over a wide range of flow rates ($Q$) and heads ($H$).

Cross-Section of a Centrifugal Pump

Operation Sequence:

  1. The volute must first be completely filled with fluid (Primed).
  2. The impeller is rotated by a motor. Centrifugal force pushes the fluid from the inner radius (the Eye) out to the outer radius.
  3. Water moves out into the volute. This creates a low-pressure zone (vacuum) at the eye.
  4. Atmospheric pressure acting on the supply reservoir forces new liquid up the suction pipe and into the eye.

The Volute: The volute is a casing with a gradually increasing cross-sectional area. As fluid collects in the volute, its velocity is deliberately slowed down ($Q = AV \implies V \downarrow$ as $A \uparrow$). This converts kinetic energy into static pressure energy, reducing energy losses before discharging into the delivery pipe.

8.4 Pump Performance & System Characteristics

For a given pump running at a constant speed, there is a unique relationship between the Head ($H$) and the Discharge ($Q$). The manufacturer provides these Performance Curves.

  • Head ($H$) vs $Q$: Typically a downward-sloping curve. As flow rate increases, the head the pump can provide decreases.
  • Efficiency ($\eta$) vs $Q$: A parabolic curve that peaks at the Best Efficiency Point (BEP).
  • Power Input ($P_i$) vs $Q$: Generally increases with flow rate.

Power of a Fluid (Power Output):

$$P_o = \gamma Q H = \rho g Q H$$

$$P_i = \frac{P_o}{\eta} = \frac{\gamma Q H}{\eta}$$

System Load Curve

When a pump is installed in a pipeline, the system requires a specific head to maintain a flow rate $Q$. By applying Bernoulli's equation from the lower reservoir (1) to the upper reservoir (2):

$$H_1 + H_{pump} = H_2 + H_L$$

$$H_{pump} = (z_2 - z_1) + H_L = H_0 + K Q^2$$

Where $H_0$ is the total static elevation lift, and $K Q^2$ represents the dynamic head losses (pipe friction and local minor losses).

Operating Point (Pump vs System Curve)

Operating Point: The exact discharge ($Q_A$) and head ($H_A$) at which a specific pump will operate when connected to a specific pipeline system. It is found at the geometric intersection of the Pump Curve and the System Curve.

Pumps in Series and Parallel

  • Parallel Pumps: Flow rates ($Q$) are added together for a given Head ($H$). Used to increase total discharge.
  • Series Pumps: Heads ($H$) are added together for a given Flow rate ($Q$). Used to overcome high static heads ($H_0$).

8.5 Turbines

Turbines extract energy from a flowing fluid and convert it into rotational mechanical energy, typically to drive an electrical generator.

Reaction Turbines

Operate under a combination of pressure and velocity changes. Water completely fills the casing and is diverted from the outer diameter through guide vanes into the inner runner.

Examples: Francis Turbine (Radial/Mixed flow), Kaplan Turbine (Axial flow). Used for low to medium heads.

Impulse Turbines

Operate entirely on the kinetic energy of a high-velocity jet striking buckets on a wheel. The pressure remains atmospheric throughout the runner.

Examples: Pelton Wheel. Used for very high elevation heads.

Force and Power on an Impulse Turbine (Pelton Wheel)

A nozzle converts the pressure head of the penstock into a high-velocity jet ($v_1$). The jet strikes a moving bucket (velocity $u$) and is deflected by an angle $\beta$ (or $180 - \theta$).

Jet Striking a Moving Pelton Bucket

Applying the steady flow momentum equation to a continuous series of moving vanes, the force exerted on the runner in the direction of motion is:

$$F = \rho Q (v_1 - u)(1 + k \cos\beta)$$

$$Power\ (P) = F \cdot u = \rho Q u (v_1 - u)(1 + k \cos\beta)$$

Where $k$ is the friction loss coefficient across the bucket (if frictionless, $k = 1$), and $\beta = 180^\circ - \theta$.

Maximum Efficiency: By differentiating Power with respect to bucket speed ($dP/du = 0$), maximum energy transfer occurs when the bucket moves at exactly half the speed of the incoming water jet: $u = 0.5 v_1$.
