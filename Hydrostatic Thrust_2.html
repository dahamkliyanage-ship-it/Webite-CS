3.0 Hydrostatic Thrust

Analyzing the forces exerted by fluids in equilibrium on submerged surfaces, exploring the magnitude, direction, and line of action on both plane and curved geometries.

3.1 Introduction to Hydrostatic Thrust

Hydrostatic Thrust: The total resultant force exerted on a submerged surface by a fluid in equilibrium.

Applications: Understanding hydrostatic thrust is vital for the structural design of dams, water tanks, lock gates, sluice gates, and submarine hulls.

To fully define the Hydrostatic Thrust (which is a Force Vector), we must determine three key properties:

  • Direction: The orientation of the force relative to the surface.
  • Magnitude: The total scalar size of the force.
  • Line of Action: The specific point where the resultant force acts (Center of Pressure).

3.2 Hydrostatic Thrust on a Plane Surface

1. Direction

Since fluids in equilibrium cannot sustain shear stresses, the force $dF$ on any infinitesimal element $dA$ is always normal (perpendicular) to the surface.

Because the surface is a flat plane, all the infinitesimal normal forces are parallel to each other. Therefore, the resultant Hydrostatic Thrust ($F$) is also normal to the surface.

2. Magnitude

The magnitude of the thrust is found by integrating the pressure over the entire submerged area.

Submerged Inclined Plane Surface Projection

Let $y$ be the distance measured along the inclined plane from the free surface axis O-O'.

The depth of any element $dA$ is $h = y \sin\theta$.

Pressure on element $dA$: $P = \rho g h = \rho g (y \sin\theta)$.

Force on element $dA$: $dF = P \cdot dA = \rho g y \sin\theta \cdot dA$.

Total Force $F = \int dF = \int \rho g y \sin\theta dA$. Since $\rho, g$, and $\sin\theta$ are constants:

$$F = \rho g \sin\theta \int y dA$$

The integral $\int y dA$ is the First Moment of Area about the axis O-O', which is equal to $A \cdot y_G$, where $y_G$ is the distance to the Centroid (Center of Gravity) from the free surface along the plane.

Substituting this back: $F = \rho g \sin\theta (A y_G) = A [(\rho g) (y_G \sin\theta)]$.

Since $y_G \sin\theta = h_G$ (the vertical depth of the centroid), the pressure at the centroid is $P_G = \rho g h_G$.

$$F = P_G \times A$$

Magnitude = [Pressure at the Centroid] × [Total Submerged Area]

3. Line of Action (Center of Pressure, C)

The resultant force $F$ acts through a point on the surface known as the Center of Pressure ($C$).

To find the distance $y_C$ from O-O' to the Center of Pressure, we take moments about the axis O-O'. The moment of the resultant force must equal the sum of the moments of the distributed forces.

$$F \cdot y_C = \int y \cdot dF$$

Substitute $F = \rho g \sin\theta \cdot A y_G$ and $dF = \rho g y \sin\theta dA$:

$$(\rho g \sin\theta \cdot A y_G) y_C = \int y (\rho g y \sin\theta dA) = \rho g \sin\theta \int y^2 dA$$

The integral $\int y^2 dA$ is the Second Moment of Area ($I_{OO}$) of the submerged surface about the free surface axis O-O'.

$$y_C = \frac{I_{OO}}{A y_G}$$

Using the Parallel Axis Theorem ($I_{OO} = I_{GG} + A y_G^2$), where $I_{GG}$ is the second moment of area about the centroidal axis parallel to O-O':

$$y_C = \frac{I_{GG} + A y_G^2}{A y_G} = y_G + \frac{I_{GG}}{A y_G}$$

Key Deductions:

  • Since $I_{GG}$ is always positive, $y_C$ is always greater than $y_G$. The Center of Pressure is always below the Centroid.
  • As the depth of immersion ($y_G$) increases, the term $\frac{I_{GG}}{A y_G}$ approaches zero. Thus, at very deep immersions, the Center of Pressure ($y_C$) approaches the Centroid ($y_G$).

Circle $\frac{\pi d^2}{4}$ $d / 2$ $$\frac{\pi d^4}{64}$$

3.4 The Pressure Diagram (Pressure Prism)

A Pressure Diagram is a visual representation showing the variation of hydrostatic pressure along a submerged surface. For a vertical or inclined plane surface, the pressure increases linearly with depth, forming a trapezoidal shape.

Pressure Diagram on a Vertical Plane Surface

For a plane surface of uniform width $b$, extending from depth $h_1$ to $h_2$, the pressure diagram is a trapezoid. The area of this pressure diagram gives the force per unit width.

$$Force / Unit\ Width = Area\ of\ the\ Pressure\ Diagram$$

$$F / b = \frac{(P_1 + P_2)}{2} \times (h_2 - h_1)$$

If the surface starts at the free surface ($h_1 = 0$, $P_1 = 0$), the pressure diagram is a triangle. The resultant force $F$ acts through the centroid of this triangular pressure diagram, which is located at a distance of $h/3$ from the base (or $2h/3$ from the free surface).

3.5 Hydrostatic Thrust on Curved Surfaces

When calculating the hydrostatic thrust on a curved surface, it is mathematically much simpler to resolve the resultant force into horizontal and vertical components rather than integrating over the complex curved geometry.

1. Horizontal Component ($F_H$)

The Horizontal Component ($F_H$) of the hydrostatic thrust on a curved surface is exactly equal to the thrust on the vertical projection of that curved surface.

It acts horizontally through the Center of Pressure of this vertically projected plane area.

2. Vertical Component ($F_V$)

The Vertical Component ($F_V$) of the hydrostatic thrust on a curved surface is equal to the weight of the fluid volume located directly above the submerged surface (extending up to the free surface).

$$F_V = Weight\ of\ Fluid = Volume \times \rho g$$

It acts downwards through the Center of Gravity of this overlying fluid volume.

Note: If the fluid is below the curved surface (pushing up), $F_V$ is equal to the weight of the imaginary volume of fluid that would exist above the surface up to the free surface level, acting upwards.

Resultant Thrust on Curved Surface

Once $F_H$ and $F_V$ are found, the magnitude and angle of the total resultant force $F$ are calculated using vector addition:

$$F = \sqrt{F_H^2 + F_V^2}$$

$$\tan\theta = \frac{F_V}{F_H}$$
