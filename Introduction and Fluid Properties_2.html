The foundational concepts of fluid mechanics, analyzing the equilibrium of fluids, matter classification, and core fluid properties such as density, viscosity, and pressure.

1.1 Fluid Mechanics Introduction

The study of the engineering aspects of fluids can be summarized as Fluid Mechanics. The engineering aspects are mainly associated with the equilibrium and motion of fluids.

Fluid Statics

Study of fluids in equilibrium.

Fluid Kinematics

Study of physical aspects of motion.

Fluid Dynamics

Study of physical aspects and the causes of motion.

Applications of Fluid Mechanics in Engineering Practice:

  • Water supply schemes
  • Irrigation schemes
  • Hydropower schemes
  • Fluid Machinery
  • Oil/gas pipelines
  • Purification of tailings
  • Aerodynamics
  • Fluid networks
  • Fluid loadings on structures
  • Lubrication
  • Biomedical Engineering

1.2 Fluids and Matter Classification

Matter exists in 3 main states: Solid, Liquid, and Gases.

Liquids and gases are commonly known as fluids. Fluids have the ability to flow. When a fluid flows, it continuously changes its shape (deforms) under the action of a shear force, no matter how small that force may be.

Definition of a Fluid: A substance that deforms continuously under the action of a shear stress, no matter how small the shear stress may be. Even a tiny force applied parallel to the surface will cause a fluid to move continuously.

Water, air, oils, fuels, and natural gases are among the fluids commonly encountered in engineering practice.

Characteristics of Fluids vs Solids

State Molecular Structure Physical Shape / Volume Behavior
Solid Closely packed molecules
Small degree of movement		 A given mass has a fixed volume and a fixed shape. Low compressibility
Permanent resistance to deformation (shear force causes a fixed deflection).
Liquid Loosely packed molecules
Greater degree of movement than in a solid		 A given mass has a fixed volume but no fixed shape (takes the shape of the container). Low compressibility
No permanent resistance to deformation (i.e. flows under shear forces).
Gas Loosely packed molecules
High degree of movement		 A given mass has no fixed volume and no fixed shape (expands to fill the container). High compressibility
No permanent resistance to deformation (i.e. flows under shear forces).

1.3 The Continuum Concept

Many of the applications of Fluid Mechanics are concerned with the bulk behavior of fluids. In such applications, a fluid is considered as a Continuum, i.e., a continuous substance filled with matter.

In a Continuum, the properties of a fluid at a point are defined for a fluid particle. A fluid particle is a small volume of fluid containing the point concerned. It is very small in macroscopic terms but very large in terms of inter-molecular distances.

Average values of properties for fluid particles are considered. The properties are assumed to vary gradually between fluid particles.

1.4 Properties of Fluids

1. Density (Mass Density) (\(\rho\))

$$Density (Mass\ Density),\ \rho = \frac{Mass\ (m)}{Volume\ (V)}$$

Units: \(kg/m^3\), \(gram/cm^3\), \(slugs/ft^3\)

Dimensions: \([\rho] = [m] / [V] = [M / L^3] = [ML^{-3}]\)

The density of liquids varies slightly with temperature, but such variations are usually neglected in many engineering applications (and a constant value is assumed).

Example: Density of water:

  • \(1000\ kg/m^3\) at \(4^\circ C \leftarrow\) maximum density
  • \(998.2\ kg/m^3\) at \(20^\circ C\) (variation less than \(0.2\%\))

Density of gases varies significantly depending on the pressure and the temperature. (e.g. Density of atmospheric air \(\approx 1.23\ kg/m^3\) at \(15^\circ C\) under atmospheric pressure).

2. Specific Weight / Unit Weight (\(\gamma\))

$$Specific\ Weight,\ \gamma = \frac{Weight\ (W)}{Volume\ (V)} \quad \text{(Weight per unit volume)}$$

Units: \(N/m^3\)

Dimensions: \([\gamma] = [W] / [V] = [MLT^{-2}] / [L^3] = [ML^{-2}T^{-2}]\)

Relationship to density: \(\gamma = \frac{W}{V} = \frac{m \cdot g}{V} = \rho \cdot g\)

Specific weight of water \(\approx 9.81 \times 10^3\ N/m^3\)

3. Relative Density / Specific Gravity (\(S\))

$$Relative\ Density,\ S = \frac{Density\ of\ a\ substance\ (\rho)}{Density\ of\ a\ standard\ substance\ (\rho_w)}$$

$$S = \frac{\rho}{\rho_{water}} = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma_{water}}$$

For liquids and solids, water is considered as the standard substance. Dimensions: Dimensionless (Ratio).

Examples: Water \(= 1.0\), Mercury \(= 13.6\), Oil \(\approx 0.8\text{ to }0.9\), Sand \(\approx 2.65\), Steel \(\approx 7.85\)

4. Vapor Pressure

Vapor pressure is due to vaporization of liquid molecules through the free surface of a liquid enclosed in a container.

Pressure due to liquid vapor above the free surface of a liquid is known as the vapor pressure. Fluid pressure must be maintained higher than the vapor pressure of a liquid to avoid boiling. (If the vapor pressure becomes equal to the ambient pressure of the liquid, boiling occurs inside the liquid mass—a phenomenon known as cavitation).

Vapor pressure of water \(= 1.7\ kN/m^2\) at \(15^\circ C\), and \(= 101.3\ kN/m^2\) (atmospheric pressure) at \(100^\circ C\) (Boiling point).

1.5 Bulk Modulus & Compressibility (\(K\))

Bulk modulus is a measure of the compressibility of a substance.

$$Bulk\ Modulus\ (K) = \frac{Change\ in\ pressure\ (\Delta P)}{Change\ in\ Volume\ / Volume\ (-\Delta V/V)}$$

$$K = - \frac{dP}{dV / V}$$

Alternatively, since \(V = m / \rho\) and mass (\(m\)) is constant, \(dV = -\frac{m}{\rho^2} d\rho\). Substituting this gives:

$$K = \rho \frac{dP}{d\rho}$$

Substances with high \(K\) values have low compressibility.
Bulk Modulus Examples:
Water \(\approx 2.0 \times 10^9\ N/m^2\)
Steel \(\approx 2.0 \times 10^{11}\ N/m^2\)
Air \(\approx 1 \times 10^5\ N/m^2\) (Highly compressible).

1.6 Surface Tension (\(\sigma\))

The free surface of a liquid behaves as a membrane under tension. Such behavior can be explained in terms of inter-molecular attraction.

Surface Tension (\(\sigma\)) is defined as Force/Length Along the line (surface).

Considering the tensile force \(F\) acting along a length \(L\): Surface Tension \(\sigma = F / L\).

Dimensions: \([\sigma] = [F] / [L] = [MLT^{-2}] / [L] = [MT^{-2}]\). Units: \(N/m\).

Surface tension of water in air \(\approx 0.073\ N/m\) at \(20^\circ C\).

Surface Tension (Capillary Rise)

Effects of surface tension are usually neglected in many engineering applications (and are only considered only in very small-scale applications). Example: Small scale physical modeling, capillary effect in small tubes.

1.7 Viscosity (\(\mu\))

When a fluid flows, forces resisting the flow are set up within the fluid. The property of a fluid that offers internal resistance to flow is known as viscosity.

Newton's Law of Viscosity

In straight and parallel flow of a fluid, the shear stress between adjacent layers is proportional to the velocity gradient perpendicular to the layers.

Velocity Profile of a Fluid Element (Linear Gradient)

Velocity gradient \(= \frac{du}{dy}\)

Shear stress, \(\tau \propto \frac{du}{dy}\)

$$\tau = \mu \frac{du}{dy}$$

The coefficient of proportionality, \(\mu\), is known as the Coefficient of Viscosity (or absolute viscosity / dynamic viscosity).

Units for \(\mu\): \(kg/ms\), \(Ns/m^2\), \(Pa \cdot s\), or \(poise\) (\(1\ kg/ms = 10\ poise\)).

Dimensions: \([\mu] = [\tau] / [du/dy] = [ML^{-1}T^{-2}] / [T^{-1}] = [ML^{-1}T^{-1}]\)

Kinematic Viscosity (\(\nu\))

$$Kinematic\ Viscosity,\ \nu = \frac{Dynamic\ Viscosity\ (\mu)}{Mass\ Density\ (\rho)}$$

Units of \(\nu\): \(m^2/s\) or \(Stokes\) (\(1\ m^2/s = 10^4\ Stokes\)).

Effect of Temperature on Viscosity

Temperature vs Viscosity

  • In liquids, viscosity is mainly caused by inter-molecular attraction. The viscosity of liquids decreases significantly with an increase in temperature.
  • In gases, viscosity is mainly due to momentum exchange between molecules. The viscosity of gases increases slightly with an increase in temperature.

Fluids in which the coefficient of viscosity is constant are known as Newtonian fluids. Most fluids encountered in fluid mechanics applications (like water, air, light oils) are considered Newtonian.

1.8 Pressure

A fluid exerts a force on any surface which it is in contact with it.

Pressure (\(P\)) = Normal Force (\(F\)) / Area (\(A\))

$$P = \frac{F_n}{A} = \lim_{\Delta A \to 0} \frac{\Delta F_n}{\Delta A}$$

Dimensions: \([P] = [F_n] / [A] = [MLT^{-2}] / [L^2] = [ML^{-1}T^{-2}]\)

Units: \(N/m^2\) or \(Pascal\) (\(Pa\)).
