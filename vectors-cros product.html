Cross Product of Vectors (Vector Product)

Definition: Let $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$ be two vectors. Then the cross product of two vectors $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$ is denoted by $\underline{a} \times \underline{b}$ and is defined by:
$$ \underline{a} \times \underline{b} = |\underline{a}||\underline{b}|\sin\theta \underline{n} $$
where $\theta$ ($0 \le \theta \le \pi$) is the angle between $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$ and $\underline{n}$ is the unit normal vector to $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$.

Figure 1: Cross Product Representation

Note. The direction of $\underline{n}$ is given by the right-hand rule. Unlike the dot product which yields a scalar, the cross product generates a completely new vector that is perpendicular to the plane formed by the initial two vectors.

Properties of the cross product

Theorem: Let $\underline{a}$, $\underline{b}$, and $\underline{c}$ be vectors. They adhere to the following properties:
  • For any given two vectors $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$, $\underline{a} \times \underline{b} = -(\underline{b} \times \underline{a})$. (This means the cross product is strictly anti-commutative).
  • For any given three vectors $\underline{a}$, $\underline{b}$, and $\underline{c}$, $\underline{a} \times (\underline{b} + \underline{c}) = \underline{a} \times \underline{b} + \underline{a} \times \underline{c}$ (distributive law).
  • If $\underline{a} \times \underline{b} = \underline{0}$ and $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$ are non-zero vectors then $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$ are parallel.
Basis Vector Interactions
Because parallel vectors yield a zero cross product (since $\sin(0^\circ) = 0$), the cross product of any basis vector with itself is zero:
$\underline{i} \times \underline{i} = \underline{j} \times \underline{j} = \underline{k} \times \underline{k} = 0$.
Using the right-hand rule, crossing orthogonal basis vectors yields the third basis vector:
$\underline{i} \times \underline{j} = \underline{k}, \quad \underline{j} \times \underline{k} = \underline{i}, \quad \text{and} \quad \underline{k} \times \underline{i} = \underline{j}$.

Suppose $\underline{a} = a_1\underline{i} + a_2\underline{j} + a_3\underline{k}$ and $\underline{b} = b_1\underline{i} + b_2\underline{j} + b_3\underline{k}$. Then $\underline{a} \times \underline{b}$ can be efficiently obtained using a determinant format:

$$ \underline{a} \times \underline{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \underline{i} & \underline{j} & \underline{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} a_2 & a_3 \\ b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix}\underline{i} - \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_3 \end{vmatrix}\underline{j} + \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ b_1 & b_2 \end{vmatrix}\underline{k} $$
$$ = (a_2b_3 - a_3b_2)\underline{i} - (a_1b_3 - a_3b_1)\underline{j} + (a_1b_2 - a_2b_1)\underline{k} $$

Applications

Application: Moment of a Force

The moment of a force is a real word application of cross-product. This is particularly critical in structural mechanics when analyzing the rotational tendency of forces around a specific joint or support.

If the force $\tilde{F}$ passes through the point $P$ and $\overrightarrow{OP} = \underline{r}$ then the moment of the force about $O$ denoted by $\tilde{M}$ is defined by:
$$ \tilde{M} = \underline{r} \times \tilde{F} $$
Engineering Mechanics Example: Calculating Joint Moments

Imagine a cantilever beam fixed at the origin $O$. A vertical force of $\tilde{F} = -50\underline{k}$ Newtons is applied at a distance along the beam defined by the position vector $\underline{r} = 3\underline{i} + 2\underline{j}$ meters. Find the resulting moment vector $\tilde{M}$ at the support $O$.

Using the determinant method:
$$ \tilde{M} = \underline{r} \times \tilde{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \underline{i} & \underline{j} & \underline{k} \\ 3 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -50 \end{vmatrix} $$
Expanding across the top row:
$$ \tilde{M} = ((2)(-50) - (0)(0))\underline{i} - ((3)(-50) - (0)(0))\underline{j} + ((3)(0) - (2)(0))\underline{k} $$ $$ \tilde{M} = -100\underline{i} - (-150)\underline{j} + 0\underline{k} $$ $$ \tilde{M} = -100\underline{i} + 150\underline{j} \text{ Nm} $$
This resulting vector describes the exact axis and magnitude of rotational torque applied to the structural support.
