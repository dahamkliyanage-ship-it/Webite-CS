1. The Limit of a Function & $\epsilon-\delta$ Formulation

Core Definitions & Directional Limits[cite: 17]

  • Definition of Limit: $\lim_{x\rightarrow a}f(x)=L \Leftrightarrow \forall\epsilon>0, \exists\delta>0, \forall x (0<|x-a|<\delta \Rightarrow |f(x)-L|<\epsilon)$[cite: 17].
  • Crucial Detail: $0<|x-a|$ means $x\ne a$[cite: 17]. A limit describes how the function behaves near $a$, completely ignoring whatever happens at exactly $a$[cite: 17].
  • Directional Limits: Approaching from Right ($x\rightarrow a^{+}$) implies $a
  • Two-Sided Limit Existence Theorem: A two-sided limit exists if and only if both one-sided limits exist and equal the exact same real value $L$[cite: 17].
Worked Exercises: Limit Proofs[cite: 17, 18, 19]

Exercise 1.1: Prove that $\lim_{x\rightarrow2}(2x+3)=7$[cite: 17]
Scratchwork: $|(2x+3)-7| = 2|x-2| < \epsilon \Leftrightarrow |x-2| < \frac{\epsilon}{2}$[cite: 17].
Proof: Let $\epsilon>0$. Choose $\delta=\frac{\epsilon}{2}>0$[cite: 17]. For all $x$ satisfying $0<|x-2|<\delta$, we have $|f(x)-7| = 2|x-2| < 2(\frac{\epsilon}{2}) = \epsilon$[cite: 17]. Thus, the limit is 7[cite: 18].

Exercise 1.3: Limit of Composite Functions[cite: 18]
Theorem: If $\lim_{x\rightarrow a}f(x)=L$, $\lim_{x\rightarrow b}g(x)=a$ and $g(x)\ne a$ on a punctured neighborhood $0<|x-b|<\delta_1$, then $\lim_{x\rightarrow b}f(g(x))=L$[cite: 18].
Proof concept: By chaining the $\delta$ values. Given $\epsilon>0$, we find $\delta_2$ for $f$, and use that $\delta_2$ as the epsilon for $g$ to find $\delta_3$[cite: 18]. Choosing $\delta = \min\{\delta_1, \delta_3\}$ ensures the composition stays within bounds[cite: 19].

2. Standard Benchmark Limits & The Squeeze Theorem

Key Theorems[cite: 19]

  • $\lim_{x\rightarrow a}\frac{x^{n}-a^{n}}{x-a}=na^{n-1}$[cite: 19]
  • $\lim_{x\rightarrow0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1$[cite: 19]
  • $\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}(1+\frac{a}{x})^{x}=e^{a}$[cite: 19]
  • Squeeze Theorem: Suppose $f(x)\le g(x)\le h(x)$ for all $x$ in a punctured neighborhood $0<|x-a|<\delta$[cite: 19]. If $\lim_{x\rightarrow a}f(x) = \lim_{x\rightarrow a}h(x) = L$, then $\lim_{x\rightarrow a}g(x)=L$[cite: 19].
Worked Exercises[cite: 19, 20]

Exercise 2.1: Prove that $\lim_{\theta\rightarrow0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}=1$[cite: 19]
Using geometric bounds for $0<\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}$, we have $\cos\theta < \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} < \frac{1}{\cos\theta}$[cite: 19]. Since $\lim_{\theta\rightarrow0}\cos\theta = 1$ and $\lim_{\theta\rightarrow0}\frac{1}{\cos\theta} = 1$, the Squeeze Theorem dictates the middle term must also approach 1[cite: 19, 20]. Since sine and $\theta$ are both odd functions, their ratio is even, making the limit hold symmetrically from the left as well[cite: 20].

Exercise 2.3: Show that $\lim_{x\rightarrow0}e^{1/x}$ does not exist[cite: 20]
Approaching from the right: As $x\rightarrow0^{+}$, $1/x\rightarrow\infty$, so $\lim_{x\rightarrow0^{+}}e^{1/x}=\infty$[cite: 20].
Approaching from the left: As $x\rightarrow0^{-}$, $1/x\rightarrow-\infty$, so $\lim_{x\rightarrow0^{-}}e^{1/x}=0$[cite: 20].
Because the left limit and right limit do not match, the limit does not exist[cite: 20].

3. Continuity & Sequential Characterization

Definitions & Key Theorems[cite: 20, 21]

  • Definition of Continuity: A function $f$ is continuous at $a$ iff $\lim_{x\rightarrow a}f(x)=f(a)$[cite: 20]. This requires three conditions: $f(a)$ is defined, $\lim_{x\rightarrow a}f(x)$ exists, and they are equal ($f(a^{-}) = f(a) = f(a^{+})$)[cite: 20, 21].
  • $\epsilon-\delta$ Criterion for Continuity: $\forall\epsilon>0, \exists\delta>0, \forall x (|x-a|<\delta \Rightarrow |f(x)-f(a)|<\epsilon)$[cite: 21]. Note the $0 < |x-a|$ condition is dropped[cite: 21].
  • Sequential Continuity Theorem: A function $f$ is continuous at $a \Leftrightarrow f$ is sequentially continuous at $a$[cite: 21]. This means for every sequence $x_n \rightarrow a$, the output sequence $f(x_n) \rightarrow f(a)$[cite: 21].
Exercise 3.1: Squeeze & Continuity Application[cite: 21, 22]

Show that $\lim_{x\rightarrow0}x\sin(\frac{1}{x})=0$ and define $f(0)$ so $f(x)$ is continuous[cite: 21].
Since $-1 \le \sin(1/x) \le 1$, multiplying by $|x|$ gives $-|x| \le x\sin(1/x) \le |x|$[cite: 21, 22]. Since both $-|x|$ and $|x|$ approach 0 as $x \to 0$, the Squeeze theorem dictates the limit is 0[cite: 22]. To make $f(x)$ continuous everywhere, we define $f(0) = 0$[cite: 22].
