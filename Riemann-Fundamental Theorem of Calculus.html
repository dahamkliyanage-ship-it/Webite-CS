Fundamental Theorem of Calculus I

There are two versions of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. Each says, roughly speaking, that differentiation and integration are inverse operations. In fact, the first version says "the integral of the derivative of a function is given by the function," and the second version says "the derivative of the integral of a continuous function is the function."

Theorem(Fundamental Theorem of Calculus - I):

If $g$ is a continuous function on $[a,b]$ that is differentiable on $(a,b)$, and if $g'$ is integrable on $[a,b]$, then
$$ \int_a^b g' = g(b) - g(a) $$
Proof:
For any $\epsilon > 0$, there exists a partition $P = \{a = t_0, t_1, \dots, t_n = b\}$ of $[a,b]$ such that $U(g', P) - L(g', P) < \epsilon$.

We apply the Mean Value Theorem to each interval $[t_{k-1}, t_k]$ to obtain $x_k \in (t_{k-1}, t_k)$ for which:
$$ (t_k - t_{k-1})g'(x_k) = g(t_k) - g(t_{k-1}) $$
Summing these terms yields a telescoping series:
$$ g(b) - g(a) = \sum_{k=1}^n (g(t_k) - g(t_{k-1})) = \sum_{k=1}^n (t_k - t_{k-1})g'(x_k) $$
It follows that $L(g', P) \le g(b) - g(a) \le U(g', P)$. By definition we know, $L(g', P) \le \int_a^b g' \le U(g', P)$. These above inequalities imply:
$$ \left| \int_a^b g' - (g(b) - g(a)) \right| \le U(g', P) - L(g', P) < \epsilon $$
Since $\epsilon$ is arbitrary, the condition holds.

Integration by Parts

Theorem(Integration by Parts):

If $u$ and $v$ are continuous functions on $[a,b]$ that are differentiable on $(a,b)$, and if $u'$ and $v'$ are integrable on $[a,b]$, then
$$ \int_a^b u(x)v'(x)dx + \int_a^b u'(x)v(x)dx = u(b)v(b) - u(a)v(a) $$
Proof:
Let $g = uv$; then by the product rule $g' = uv' + u'v$ and $g$ is integrable (as $u, u', v, v'$ are integrable). Now by FTC-I:
$$ \int_a^b g' = g(b) - g(a) = u(b)v(b) - u(a)v(a) $$ $$ \int_a^b (u(x)v'(x) + u'(x)v(x))dx = u(b)v(b) - u(a)v(a) $$
Splitting the integral gives the result.
Added Worked Example: Integration by Parts

Evaluate the definite integral $\int_0^{\pi} x \cos(x) dx$.

Step 1: Assign $u$ and $v'$.
Let $u(x) = x \implies u'(x) = 1$
Let $v'(x) = \cos(x) \implies v(x) = \sin(x)$

Step 2: Apply Theorem 0.5.2 (rearranged).
$$ \int_0^\pi x \cos(x) dx = [x \sin(x)]_0^\pi - \int_0^\pi 1 \cdot \sin(x) dx $$ $$ = (\pi \sin(\pi) - 0 \sin(0)) - [-\cos(x)]_0^\pi $$ $$ = 0 - (-\cos(\pi) + \cos(0)) = 0 - (-(-1) + 1) = -2 $$

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus II

Theorem(Fundamental Theorem of Calculus II):

Let $f$ be an integrable function on $[a,b]$. For $x \in [a,b]$, let
$$ F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)dt $$
Then $F$ is continuous on $[a,b]$. If $f$ is continuous at $x_0 \in (a,b)$, then $F$ is differentiable at $x_0$ and
$$ F'(x_0) = f(x_0) $$
Proof(Continuity):
Select $M > 0$ so that $|f(x)| \le M$ for all $x \in [a,b]$. If $x, y \in [a,b]$ and $|x-y| < \delta = \frac{\epsilon}{M}$ where $x < y$, then:
$$ |F(y) - F(x)| = \left| \int_x^y f(t)dt \right| \le \int_x^y |f(t)|dt \le \int_x^y M dt = M(y-x) < \epsilon $$
This shows $F$ is uniformly continuous. Further application of limits demonstrates $F'(x_0) = f(x_0)$.

Chain Rule & Function Boundaries

Theorem(Leibniz Integral Rule):

If $f$ is continuous on an open interval $I$ containing the values of differentiable functions $y = a(x)$ and $y = b(x)$, then:
$$ \frac{d}{dx} \int_{a(x)}^{b(x)} f(t)dt = f(b(x))b'(x) - f(a(x))a'(x) $$
Proof:
Let $F(u) = \int_{t_0}^u f(t)dt$. From FTC-II, $F'(u) = f(u)$. Consider:
$$ \frac{d}{dx} \int_{a(x)}^{b(x)} f(t)dt = \frac{d}{dx} \left[ \int_{a(x)}^{t_0} f(t)dt + \int_{t_0}^{b(x)} f(t)dt \right] $$ $$ = \frac{d}{dx} [F(b(x)) - F(a(x))] $$
Applying the standard chain rule yields $F'(b(x))b'(x) - F'(a(x))a'(x) = f(b(x))b'(x) - f(a(x))a'(x)$.
Added Worked Example: Using Theorem

Find the derivative of the accumulation function with respect to $x$: $\frac{d}{dx} \int_{2x}^{x^2} e^{t^2} dt$

Step 1: Identify components.
$f(t) = e^{t^2}$
$b(x) = x^2 \implies b'(x) = 2x$
$a(x) = 2x \implies a'(x) = 2$

Step 2: Apply the formula.
$$ = f(x^2)(2x) - f(2x)(2) $$ $$ = e^{(x^2)^2}(2x) - e^{(2x)^2}(2) $$ $$ = 2x e^{x^4} - 2 e^{4x^2} $$

Change of Variable

Theorem(Change of Variable / Substitution Rule):

Let $u$ be a differentiable function on an open interval $J$ such that $u'$ is continuous, and let $I$ be an open interval such that $u(x) \in I$ for all $x \in J$. If $f$ is continuous on $I$, then $f \circ u$ is continuous on $J$ and:
$$ \int_a^b f(u(x)) u'(x)dx = \int_{u(a)}^{u(b)} f(u)du $$
