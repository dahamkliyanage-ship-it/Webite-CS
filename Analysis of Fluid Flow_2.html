7.0 Analysis of Fluid Flow

Applying the fundamental laws of mechanics—Conservation of Mass, Energy, and Momentum—using Control Volume analysis to solve practical fluid dynamics problems.

7.1 Concept of the Control Volume

In solid mechanics, we typically track a specific mass of material (a system). In fluid mechanics, because fluids continuously deform and move, it is much easier to define a specific, fixed region in space and observe the fluid flowing through it. This region is called a Control Volume (CV).

  • Control Volume (CV): A defined volume in space through which a fluid flows. It can be real or imaginary, fixed in shape or deformable, stationary or moving.
  • Control Surface (CS): The boundary of the Control Volume. Mass, momentum, and energy cross the Control Surface.

Control Volume in a Converging Pipe

The core Laws of Mechanics (Conservation of Mass, Energy, and Momentum) are fundamentally applied to the flow through the boundaries of the Control Volume.

7.2 Conservation of Mass (Continuity Equation)

Principle: Mass cannot be created or destroyed (except in nuclear processes).

For a Control Volume, the general conservation of mass equation dictates that the rate of mass entering minus the rate of mass leaving equals the rate of change of mass inside the Control Volume.

$$ \text{Mass inflow rate} - \text{Mass outflow rate} = \text{Rate of change of Mass in the CV} $$

$$ \frac{dm_i}{dt} - \frac{dm_o}{dt} = \frac{dm}{dt} $$

For an Incompressible Flow (density $\rho$ is essentially constant), the total mass inside a fixed CV cannot change, so $\frac{dm}{dt} = 0$. Therefore:

$$ \frac{dm_i}{dt} = \frac{dm_o}{dt} \implies \dot{m}_i = \dot{m}_o $$

Since mass flow rate $\dot{m} = \rho \times Q$ (where $Q$ is the volumetric flow rate) and density $\rho$ is constant, it cancels out:

$$ Q_{in} = Q_{out} $$

$$ A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2 = \text{constant} $$

This is the standard Continuity Equation for incompressible 1D flow. It mathematically proves that as the cross-sectional area ($A$) decreases, the velocity ($V$) must proportionally increase to maintain a constant volumetric flow rate ($Q$).

7.3 Conservation of Energy (Bernoulli's Equation)

Principle: Energy cannot be created or destroyed, but it can be transformed from one form to another. In fluid flow, energy converted into certain forms (like heat due to friction) is considered a "loss" to the mechanical system.

In fluid mechanics, we express the primary forms of mechanical energy per unit weight, yielding terms called "Heads" (measured in length units, like meters):

  • Potential Head ($z$): Energy derived from elevation above a datum.
  • Pressure Head ($\frac{P}{\rho g}$): Flow work done by the fluid's static pressure.
  • Velocity Head ($\frac{V^2}{2g}$): Kinetic energy of the moving fluid.

Bernoulli's Theorem: In a steady, incompressible, frictionless (ideal) flow along a streamline, the Total Head remains constant.

$$ z + \frac{P}{\rho g} + \frac{V^2}{2g} = \text{Total Head} = \text{constant} $$

$$ z_1 + \frac{P_1}{\rho g} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} = z_2 + \frac{P_2}{\rho g} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} $$

The sum of the elevation and pressure terms ($z + \frac{P}{\rho g}$) is known specifically as the Piezometric Head.

Modifications for Real Fluid Flow (Steady Flow Energy Equation)

In real-world fluid flow, energy is inevitably lost due to pipe friction and turbulence. We introduce a Head Loss term ($H_L$). Furthermore, mechanical energy can be actively added to the system by pumps ($H_p$) or extracted from the system by turbines ($H_t$).

$$ z_1 + \frac{P_1}{\rho g} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + H_p = z_2 + \frac{P_2}{\rho g} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + H_t + H_L $$

Bernoulli Applications: Siphon and Nozzle

Applying Bernoulli to a Siphon: A siphon is a tube that raises a liquid over an elevation and discharges it at a lower level. Taking Point 1 at the free surface of the upper tank ($P_1 = P_{atm} = 0$, $V_1 \approx 0$) and Point 2 at the exit jet ($P_2 = P_{atm} = 0$), Bernoulli's equation simplifies entirely to $z_1 = z_2 + \frac{V_2^2}{2g}$. Therefore, the discharge velocity is purely a function of the elevation difference:

$$V_2 = \sqrt{2g(z_1 - z_2)}$$

7.4 Conservation of Momentum

Forces exerted on solid bodies due to the dynamic flow of fluids can be determined by applying Newton's 2nd Law ($\Sigma F = \frac{dM}{dt}$) to a control volume.

Steady Flow Momentum Equation:

The net force acting on the fluid within the control volume equals the momentum outflow rate minus the momentum inflow rate.

$$ F_s = \dot{M}_{out} - \dot{M}_{in} $$

Where $F_s$ is the sum of all external forces acting on the fluid (pressure forces, body/weight forces, and reaction forces from the solid boundaries). Momentum flow rate is calculated as $\dot{M} = \dot{m}V = \rho Q V$.

By Newton's 3rd Law, the force exerted by the fluid on the solid boundary is equal and opposite to the reaction force exerted by the solid boundary on the fluid.

Application 1: Force Exerted by a Jet Striking a Plate

Consider a horizontal jet of water striking a stationary flat plate. The jet is deflected radially, causing a complete destruction of its forward momentum.

Applying the Momentum Equation in the x-direction:

$$ \Sigma F_x = \dot{m}(V_{out,x} - V_{in,x}) $$

$$ -R_x = \rho Q (0 - V_1) \implies R_x = \rho Q V_1 $$

The force exerted on the plate is $F_{plate} = R_x = \rho Q V_1$. If the plate is angled at $\theta$ relative to the jet, the normal force on the plate becomes $F_n = \rho Q V_1 \sin\theta$.

Application 2: Force on a Pipe Bend

A fluid flowing through a pipe bend changes both the direction and often the magnitude of its velocity. This change in momentum vectors requires a significant reaction force to hold the bend in place (which is why large water mains require heavy concrete thrust blocks at corners).

Control Volume for a Pipe Bend

Applying Momentum Equation for Pipe Bend:

Let $F_x$ and $F_y$ be the internal reaction forces exerted by the pipe wall on the fluid.

  • x-direction: $P_1 A_1 - P_2 A_2 \cos\theta + F_x = \rho Q (V_2 \cos\theta - V_1)$
  • y-direction: $-P_2 A_2 \sin\theta + F_y - W = \rho Q (V_2 \sin\theta - 0)$
    (Where $W$ is the weight of fluid inside the CV, often negligible if the bend is purely in a horizontal plane)

Solve for $F_x$ and $F_y$. The resultant force required to anchor the pipe block is equal in magnitude but opposite in direction: $R = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2}$.
