5.0 Relative Equilibrium of Fluids

Analyzing the behavior of fluids in vessels subjected to constant linear acceleration or constant angular rotation, where fluid particles are at rest relative to the container.

5.1 Introduction to Relative Equilibrium

  • A fluid contained in a vessel is not affected if the vessel moves at a constant velocity (acceleration = 0). The fluid free surface remains perfectly horizontal.
  • However, if the vessel moves at a constant acceleration, that acceleration is transmitted to the fluid. The fluid particles will move to new positions and then remain in those new positions in equilibrium relative to the moving vessel. Such an equilibrium state is known as the Relative Equilibrium of a fluid.
  • Similarly, if a vessel containing a fluid is rotated at a constant angular velocity, the fluid will reach a relative equilibrium position and rotate with the vessel as a rigid body. The fluid is said to be in Forced Vortex Motion under such conditions.

Key Principle: As there is no relative movement between the fluid particles themselves once relative equilibrium is reached, no shear forces are present in the fluid. All existing forces are normal to the surfaces on which they act.

Hence, the fundamental principles of 'Fluid Statics' can be used to study fluids in 'Relative Equilibrium'.

5.2 Relative Equilibrium under Linear Acceleration

Consider a fluid in relative equilibrium under linear accelerations $a_x, a_y,$ and $a_z$ in the $x, y,$ and $z$ directions respectively. Let $P$ be the pressure at a point $A(x, y, z)$.

Since $P = f(x, y, z)$, the total differential of pressure is:

$$dp = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} dx + \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} dy + \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} dz \quad \text{--- (1)}$$

By applying Newton's 2nd Law ($F = m \cdot a$) to a small fluid control volume of dimensions $\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta z$ around point A, and considering the pressure gradients:

  • x-direction: $\left( P - \frac{\partial P}{\partial x}\frac{\Delta x}{2} \right)\Delta y \Delta z - \left( P + \frac{\partial P}{\partial x}\frac{\Delta x}{2} \right)\Delta y \Delta z = (\rho \Delta x \Delta y \Delta z) a_x \implies \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} = -\rho a_x$
  • y-direction: Similarly, $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = -\rho a_y$
  • z-direction: Including gravity acting downwards: $\frac{\partial P}{\partial z} = -\rho (a_z + g)$

Substituting these partial derivatives back into equation (1):

$$dp = -\rho a_x dx - \rho a_y dy - \rho (a_z + g) dz$$

Integrating for an incompressible fluid ($\rho = \text{constant}$):

$$P = -\rho a_x x - \rho a_y y - \rho (a_z + g) z + C'$$

5.3 Shape of the Free Surface & Isobars

A surface of constant pressure is known as an isobar. Along the free surface, the pressure is constant and equal to atmospheric pressure ($P = P_{atm} = \text{const} = 0$ in gauge).

Setting $P = 0$ in the integrated equation gives:

$$0 = -\rho a_x x - \rho a_y y - \rho (a_z + g) z + C'$$

This is in the mathematical form of $Ax + By + Cz + D = 0$, which is the Equation of a Plane Surface.

Hence, the free surface of a fluid undergoing constant linear acceleration remains a flat, plane surface in 3D space, although it may be tilted.

Inclination with the Horizontal Plane

If $\theta_x$ and $\theta_y$ are the angles of inclination of the free surface with respect to the horizontal $x$ and $y$ axes respectively, then $\tan \theta_x = \frac{dz}{dx}$ and $\tan \theta_y = \frac{dz}{dy}$.

Differentiating the free surface equation with respect to $x$ (treating $y$ as constant):

$$-\rho a_x (1) - 0 - \rho (a_z + g) \frac{dz}{dx} = 0$$

$$\frac{dz}{dx} = \tan \theta_x = -\frac{a_x}{a_z + g}$$

Similarly: $$\frac{dz}{dy} = \tan \theta_y = -\frac{a_y}{a_z + g}$$

5.4 Relative Equilibrium under Horizontal Acceleration

Consider a fluid accelerating strictly horizontally in the x-direction ($a_x \neq 0$, and $a_y = a_z = 0$).

The equation of the free surface simplifies to: $-\rho a_x x - \rho g z = C'$.

The inclination of the free surface is:

$$\tan \theta_x = -\frac{a_x}{g}$$

Note: The negative sign indicates that for a positive acceleration ($a_x > 0$), the surface slopes downward in the direction of acceleration. (Fluid piles up at the back of the tank).

Horizontal Acceleration vs Equilibrium

Vertical Pressure Distribution

To find the pressure difference between two vertical points 1 and 2 separated by height $h$ ($x_1 = x_2$, $z_1 - z_2 = h$):

$P_1 = -\rho a_x x_1 - \rho g z_1 + C'$

$P_2 = -\rho a_x x_2 - \rho g z_2 + C'$

$P_2 - P_1 = -\rho a_x (x_2 - x_1) - \rho g (z_2 - z_1) = 0 - \rho g (-h)$

$$P_2 = P_1 + \rho g h$$

Crucial Concept: The vertical pressure distribution in a horizontally accelerating tank is purely hydrostatic. Pressure at any depth is simply $P = \rho g h$, where $h$ is measured vertically from the tilted free surface.

5.5 Relative Equilibrium under Vertical Acceleration

Consider a fluid experiencing purely vertical acceleration ($a_z \neq 0$, $a_x = a_y = 0$). This occurs in an elevator.

The equation of the free surface: $0 + 0 - \rho (a_z + g) z = C' \implies z = \text{constant}$.

Since $z$ is constant, the free surface remains perfectly horizontal. There is no inclination with the horizontal plane.

Vertical Pressure Distribution

Applying the pressure equation between two vertical points separated by depth $h$:

$P_2 - P_1 = -\rho(a_z + g)(z_2 - z_1) = -\rho(a_z + g)(-h)$

$$P_2 = P_1 + \rho (g + a_z) h$$

$$P = \rho h (g \pm a_z)$$

Note on Signs: Use $+a_z$ for upward acceleration (or downward deceleration). Use $-a_z$ for downward acceleration (or upward deceleration). If the fluid is in freefall ($a_z = -g$), the pressure $P = 0$ everywhere.

5.6 Forced Vortex Motion (Rotation about a vertical axis)

If a cylindrical vessel containing fluid is rotated at a constant angular velocity $\omega$ (rad/s) about its central vertical axis, the fluid eventually moves as a solid body. This is a forced vortex.

In cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$, the acceleration towards the center is the centripetal acceleration: $a_r = -r \omega^2$.

Applying Newton's second law radially: $\frac{\partial P}{\partial r} = \rho r \omega^2$. Vertically, we still have $\frac{\partial P}{\partial z} = -\rho g$.

Integrating these gives the pressure equation for a rotating fluid:

$$P = \frac{\rho \omega^2 r^2}{2} - \rho g z + C$$

Equation of the Free Surface

At the free surface, $P = 0$. Let the origin $(0,0)$ be the lowest point of the free surface depression (the vertex of the paraboloid).

At $r = 0, z = 0$, we find $C = 0$. Thus, the equation of the free surface is:

$$z = \frac{\omega^2 r^2}{2g}$$

This is the equation of a Paraboloid. The free surface forms a parabolic curve. The height $z$ of the fluid at any radius $r$ from the center increases with the square of the angular velocity.
