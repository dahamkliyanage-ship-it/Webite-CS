The Dot Product of Vectors (Scalar Product)

Definition: Let $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$ be two vectors. Then the dot product of two vectors $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$ is denoted by $\underline{a} \cdot \underline{b}$ and is defined by
$$ \underline{a} \cdot \underline{b} = |\underline{a}||\underline{b}|\cos\theta $$
where $\theta$ ($0 \le \theta \le \pi$) is the angle between $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$.
Example: Calculating the Dot Product

Suppose you have two force vectors pulling on a single anchor point.
  • Vector $\underline{a}$ has a magnitude of $5$ Newtons ($|\underline{a}| = 5$).
  • Vector $\underline{b}$ has a magnitude of $8$ Newtons ($|\underline{b}| = 8$).
  • The angle $\theta$ between them is $60^\circ$.

To find the dot product, apply Definition 2.1.1:
$$ \underline{a} \cdot \underline{b} = (5)(8)\cos(60^\circ) $$ $$ \underline{a} \cdot \underline{b} = 40 \times 0.5 = 20 $$
Note: The result of a dot product is always a scalar (a standard number), not a vector.
Theorem: Suppose $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$ are non-zero vectors. Then $\underline{a}$ and $\underline{b}$ are orthogonal if and only if $\underline{a} \cdot \underline{b} = 0$.

Context: "Orthogonal" means the vectors meet at exactly a $90^\circ$ angle (perpendicular). Because $\cos(90^\circ) = 0$, the entire dot product equation naturally equals $0$.



Properties of the dot product

Theorem: Let $\underline{a}$, $\underline{b}$, and $\underline{c}$ be any vectors in the same vector space. Then they satisfy the following laws.
  • $\underline{a} \cdot \underline{b} = \underline{b} \cdot \underline{a}$ (commutative law)
  • $\underline{a} \cdot (\underline{b} + \underline{c}) = \underline{a} \cdot \underline{b} + \underline{a} \cdot \underline{c}$ (distributive law)
Example: Using the Distributive Law

We can use the distributive property alongside the rules established in Exercise 1 to easily calculate the dot product of vectors expressed in component form.

Let $\underline{a} = 2\underline{i} + 3\underline{j}$ and $\underline{b} = 4\underline{i} - \underline{j}$.

Using the distributive law:
$$ \underline{a} \cdot \underline{b} = (2\underline{i} + 3\underline{j}) \cdot (4\underline{i} - \underline{j}) $$ $$ = (2\underline{i} \cdot 4\underline{i}) - (2\underline{i} \cdot \underline{j}) + (3\underline{j} \cdot 4\underline{i}) - (3\underline{j} \cdot \underline{j}) $$
Since orthogonal components cancel out to zero ($\underline{i} \cdot \underline{j} = 0$) and identical components multiply to one ($\underline{i} \cdot \underline{i} = 1$):
$$ = (2 \times 4)(1) - 0 + 0 - (3 \times 1)(1) $$ $$ = 8 - 3 = 5 $$
